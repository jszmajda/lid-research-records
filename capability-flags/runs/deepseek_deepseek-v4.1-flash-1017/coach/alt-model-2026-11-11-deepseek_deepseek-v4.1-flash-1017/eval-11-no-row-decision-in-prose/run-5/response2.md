**F2 high — the in-memory queue loses accepted jobs on restart, working against the project's own promise tenet.** `job-queue-design.md:14` says the queue is "held entirely in process memory" and that on restart "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same doc's Context (line 10) names the operating conditions that trigger this — "the workshop mini-PC, which takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." The HLD opens its tenets (line 20) with "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," and the goal on line 26 is "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." Under a described condition — an overnight OS update reboots the mini-PC — every queued-but-undispatched overnight job vanishes, the member is asleep, and morning pickup yields nothing. That is the exact scenario the product exists for. This is a *tenet cascade* problem: the design mechanism quietly defeats an HLD tenet, and no Decisions row records the trade, so a cold reader cannot tell whether the team weighed durability against simplicity or simply never noticed the collision. Left as is, the gap compounds: specs written next will encode the lossy behavior, and tests written from them will lock it in. Your remedy is a genuine choice between three options, and I'd rather you pick than have me pick: **change the design** (persist accepted jobs — a small SQLite table or append-only on-disk journal fits the "mini-PC stays small" tenet and the content-hash file store already on disk), **record the exception** (a Decisions row accepting job loss, naming the "job is a promise" tenet it trades against, and why), or **revise the tenet** to say what you actually guarantee (e.g. "a dispatched job is a promise"). I'd lean toward persisting the queue, because the file store already exists and the tenet is the product's core promise — but that's your call, and any of the three is defensible if the record shows you chose it.

**F6 medium — the in-memory choice itself is a live decision with no captured row.** Distinct from the tenet conflict: `job-queue-design.md:14` states the in-memory decision as architecture prose, and the Decisions & Alternatives table (lines 29–33) covers scheduling order, identifiers, and upload size but never addresses durability. Under *decision capture* (the keep-side of *docs carry current intent* — a decision that stays live after it lands belongs in the Decisions table with its rejected alternative and accepted cost), this is the kind of call a cold reader will still question: "why in memory, when the tenet says jobs are promises?" and "what did persistence cost that made you skip it?" Neither is reconstructable from the page. The remedy is the smallest capture that settles it — usually a fuller row in `job-queue-design.md` naming the alternative (persist to SQLite/journal), the accepted cost, and the operating condition accepted (job loss on restart). A decision doc would be overkill here unless the durability debate ran deep enough that the team wants the full option space on record; if you change the design instead, that row simply records the chosen persistence approach and why in-memory was rejected. Note this remedy and F2's "record the exception" overlap — one row can close both — but you may still prefer to fix F2 by changing the design rather than recording it.

**F7 medium — fairness is declared as a goal but has no mechanism.** HLD line 27 sets the goal "Fair access to the popular printer classes," and `job-queue-design.md:25` offers only "Two priority classes: staff and member. Within a class, first-in-first-out." FIFO is order-fairness per request, not access-fairness across members: nothing limits how many jobs one member can queue, so a member who uploads five long jobs in the evening owns a popular class until morning while others wait. This is a *tenet cascade* gap — the goal and the mechanism never share wording, but under the conditions the docs describe (evening batch uploads, a few popular classes) the design defeats the stated goal. As with F2, the remedy is yours to choose: **change the design** (a per-member cap, a round-robin across members, or a fairness-aware ordering), **record the exception** (a Decisions row naming the fairness goal as best-effort and why FIFO was chosen instead), or **revise the goal** (drop "fair" to something FIFO actually delivers, like "predictable first-come access"). No spec currently states fairness either, so whichever you pick should land as a `QUEUE-` spec — otherwise the goal stays unexecutable.
