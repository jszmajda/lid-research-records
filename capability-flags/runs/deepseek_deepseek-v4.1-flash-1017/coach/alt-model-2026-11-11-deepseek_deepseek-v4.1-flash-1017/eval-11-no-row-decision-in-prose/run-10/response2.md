**F2 — High. The "promise" tenet and the in-memory queue pull in opposite directions.** The HLD's Tenets section says "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" (`docs/high-level-design.md:22`), but `job-queue-design.md:12` states that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same LLD's Context names the operating condition that triggers this: "the workshop mini-PC... takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" (`job-queue-design.md:11`). Under *tenet cascade* — design nodes should lean the way the HLD's tenets say — this is a mechanism that defeats a tenet under a condition the docs themselves describe. The cost of leaving it: a member who uploads in the evening and walks away can lose the job to an overnight reboot and only find out the next morning when the part isn't there — exactly the failure the tenet exists to prevent. The remedy is your choice among three: change the design (persist accepted jobs so they survive restart), record the exception in the Decisions table naming the tenet traded against and why the in-memory tradeoff is accepted, or revise the tenet to something the design actually keeps. Recording the exception would also close F3 in one edit — but you may still prefer a design change or a tenet revision.

**F3 — High. The in-memory decision has no Decisions row.** The `job-queue` Decisions & Alternatives table covers scheduling order, job identifiers, and upload size limit (`job-queue-design.md:20-22`) but not the choice to hold the queue entirely in process memory — the choice with the largest user-visible cost. Under *decision capture* — a decision that stays live once landed should be reconstructable from its row — a cold reader of the landed design would still question this choice (they'd want to know why durability was given up and what was accepted in return), but the rationale survives only in the Architecture prose. The cost of leaving it: the next session can't tell whether durability was rejected deliberately or overlooked, so it may "fix" it against intent or preserve it without understanding the tradeoff. Recommend adding a row to the Decisions table naming the chosen option (in-memory queue), the rejected alternative (persist the queue to the SQLite file the registry already uses), and the accepted cost (accepted-but-undispatched jobs lost on restart; members resubmit). A decision doc is an option only if you want the full option space on record — for this choice a row is the right size.

**F4 — Medium. "Suspect" is named but never defined.** `job-queue-design.md:17` says a failed dispatch "returns the job to the head of its class queue and the printer is marked suspect," but neither the LLD nor the registry LLD defines what suspect means: does the registry skip suspect printers, does the queue retry the same printer, is there a retry limit? The registry's states are "idle, busy, unreachable" (`printer-registry-design.md:11`), with no suspect. Under *LLDs close enough of the solution space*, this leaves the failure path open to interpretation — and it cascades across a node boundary: "suspect" is a queue concept with no home in the registry. The cost: two sessions implementing dispatch failure can make different choices about retry behavior, and a printer that rejects a bad job could loop forever. Try resolving where the state belongs — likely a registry state the queue consults — and add the retry limit to the dispatch-failure EARS spec; if this crosses into registry territory, pause and confirm the registry change rather than assuming it.

**Also worth pairing with F1:** neither the restart-loss behavior nor the "suspect" path has a spec. `QUEUE-004` covers rebuilding *running* printer state on startup but says nothing about accepted-but-undispatched jobs, and no spec fixes a retry limit. Once you decide the design question in F2/F3, write the EARS specs first and the tests against them — that's where the *tests before code* principle earns its keep.
