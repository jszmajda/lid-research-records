The conflict is in `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14`: accepted jobs live only in process memory, so a restart discards every job that has not dispatched. The same paragraph preserves G-code by content hash, but preserving a file is not preserving the member's submission: its target printer class, queue position, submitter/priority, acceptance time, and status are gone. A member who uploaded before an unattended update or power blip must notice the loss and submit again.

That directly works against the HLD tenet in `docs/high-level-design.md:20`: “A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away.” Under *tenet cascade* — leaf mechanisms should honor the HLD’s declared lean, or explicitly record why they do not — the queue behavior makes unattended overnight use unreliable precisely under an operating condition the queue design calls out. It also makes queue visibility misleading: a member may reasonably see an accepted job, while a restart silently removes it.

I would treat this as an intentional product choice to settle, rather than an implementation detail. You have three valid paths:

1. Change the design to persist accepted job records before acknowledging acceptance. On startup, reload queued jobs, reconcile jobs believed to be dispatched by polling printers, and resume dispatch for the remaining queued work. This best preserves the existing “upload and walk away” tenet.
2. Keep the in-memory design, but record a Decisions & Alternatives entry that explicitly names the submitted-job tenet being traded off, why the small-service constraint outweighs it, and the accepted user-facing consequence. Then revise the HLD tenet or goal so the product does not promise durability it deliberately does not provide.
3. Change the product boundary: for example, only call a job “accepted” after it has been handed to a printer, with earlier uploads clearly labelled as temporary pending submissions. This is still a tenet change in practice, but may be suitable if queue durability is outside the intended service scope.

If the current HLD is the intended product promise, I would choose the first path. The smallest design change is a durable queue-record store alongside the already-documented SQLite-backed registry, rather than introducing another service. Then add EARS specs covering durable acceptance, restart recovery, and reconciliation of an in-flight dispatch so tests can establish the promise before implementation.
