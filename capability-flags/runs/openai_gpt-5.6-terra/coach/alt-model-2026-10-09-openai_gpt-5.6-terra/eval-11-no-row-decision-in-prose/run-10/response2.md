**F2 — High. The current queue design breaks the project’s submitted-job promise after a restart.** `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14` says accepted but undispatched jobs live only in process memory and are lost after a restart, while the same document explicitly anticipates power blips. That conflicts with the HLD tenet in `docs/high-level-design.md:20`: “A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away.” Under *tenet cascade* — leaf designs should carry the HLD’s stated preferences into their mechanisms — this is consequential: a member may submit an evening job, receive acceptance, and discover only the next day that it vanished. The on-disk content-addressed G-code copy does not resolve that, because the queue record, target class, priority, submission order, and dispatch state are the data needed to resume it.

There are three valid directions; the right one is a product choice:

1. **Change the design to persist accepted jobs.** Store the job record and queue state durably at acceptance time, then reconstruct undispatched jobs on startup. The subsequent LLD/spec work should define when acceptance becomes durable, how an in-flight dispatch is reconciled after restart, and whether printer-side job identity supports safe retry. This best preserves the existing “upload and walk away” tenet.
2. **Keep the in-memory queue and record an explicit exception.** Add a `Decisions & Alternatives` row in the queue LLD naming the submitted-job promise being traded off, why a durable queue is not justified, and the accepted member-facing cost. The UI and EARS specs would then need to make the limitation explicit before or at submission, rather than presenting acceptance as a durable promise. This also closes the decision-capture gap.
3. **Revise the HLD tenet.** If the actual intended promise is only “the service will attempt to dispatch while it remains running,” narrow the tenet so the architecture does not promise durable acceptance. This is appropriate only if that weaker member experience is genuinely acceptable.

The existing mini-PC constraint does not inherently require an in-memory queue: the registry already uses a single SQLite file, so a small durable job store is compatible with the project’s “service stays small” tenet.
