**Tenet conflict.** `docs/high-level-design.md:20` says that submitting a job is a promise and members should be able to upload and walk away; the overnight-print goal at `docs/high-level-design.md:26` reinforces that expectation. Meanwhile, `job-queue-design.md:10` explicitly anticipates unattended OS updates and power interruptions, while `job-queue-design.md:14` says every accepted-but-undispatched job disappears after either event. Under *tenet cascade*, leaf-level mechanisms should support project-level promises unless an intentional exception is recorded. The practical failure is straightforward: the service acknowledges a job, the member goes offline, the mini-PC restarts, and nobody knows the job needs resubmission until the expected print is missing.

**Decision capture.** Volatile queue storage is also absent from the Decisions & Alternatives table, even though smaller choices such as identifier format and upload limits are recorded there. Under *docs carry current intent*, a consequential choice that a cold reader would question should preserve the rejected alternative and accepted cost. As written, a future implementer cannot tell whether volatility is an intentional simplicity tradeoff, a temporary shortcut, or an overlooked contradiction. The smallest remedy is a Decisions row explaining the chosen durability contract, the alternative, and which tenet or goal is being traded off.

**Missing behavioral contract.** `QUEUE-004` only requires startup polling to reconstruct jobs already running on printers. No spec says what happens to accepted queued jobs, when an upload becomes “accepted,” or whether an acceptance response guarantees survival across restart. Under *LLDs close enough of the solution space*, two reasonable implementers should not be able to produce incompatible durability semantics. This ambiguity must be resolved in the design and EARS specs before tests or implementation begin.

I recommend deciding the product contract before choosing a storage mechanism. LID leaves three legitimate remedies:

1. **Make acceptance durable.** Define a job as accepted only after its G-code and queue metadata are durably stored. On startup, restore queued jobs and reconcile dispatched or running jobs by polling printers. SQLite is an obvious option given the existing registry design, but whether the queue shares that database or owns another store is a downstream design choice.
2. **Keep the volatile queue and record an explicit exception.** Add a Decisions row naming the unattended-job tenet and overnight goal being traded away, explain why the simplicity benefit justifies that cost, and make the UI communicate that accepted jobs may disappear after restart. Recording this exception also resolves the missing decision-capture finding.
3. **Revise the project intent.** Change the HLD so submission is explicitly best-effort rather than a durable promise. The queue design can then remain volatile, but the overnight-print goal and user expectations must be rewritten to match.

If you choose durable acceptance, the cascade should update `job-queue-design.md`, add EARS requirements for durable acknowledgment and restart recovery, then add tests before implementation. If you choose volatility, the cascade should update the HLD or record the exception, clarify acceptance semantics in the LLD, and add specs for the user-visible loss behavior.

Which contract do you want: does an “accepted” job survive a service restart, or is submission intentionally best-effort?
