**F1: Accepted jobs live only in memory, and a restart drops them.** The job-queue design says the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and that jobs accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart "are gone, and members resubmit them" (`docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md`, Architecture). That collides with the HLD's first tenet, "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" (`docs/high-level-design.md`, Tenets), and with the goal "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." The job-queue Context names the condition that triggers the problem: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." A restart at 3 a.m. loses the evening's uploads, and members learn about it the next morning, with nobody there to resubmit. The principle at stake is *tenets as tie-breakers*: a tenet is supposed to decide the call when two designs are defensible and no spec covers it, so a mechanism that works against a tenet should either change or be recorded as a deliberate exception. Nothing records this one. The Decisions table has three rows, none about storage. The cost is that a cold reader, or a future session deciding whether to add persistence, has to reconstruct a trade-off nobody wrote down.

The design also sets up an asymmetry a reader will notice. The printer registry persists to a single SQLite file, which is the same mini-PC and the same kind of store. The queue, which is what members actually depend on, doesn't persist at all. The registry's Decisions row says "nothing else needs concurrent writers," which reads as the reason SQLite was chosen for the registry and doesn't address the queue. A cold reader would ask why the less important state is durable and the more important one is not.

**F4 is the tenet-ordering half of the same issue.** The HLD lists three tenets without order. Tenet 1 (the promise) and tenet 3 ("The mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small") both apply here, and tenet 3 is probably the reason the queue is in memory. With no order, a future session can't tell which tenet wins, so the in-memory choice looks defensible under one tenet and wrong under the other. The HLD template asks for tenets ordered so that the higher one wins on conflict. Ordering them would settle this case and any similar ones.

**What I'd recommend**

My recommendation is to persist accepted jobs. Put them in a table in the SQLite file the registry already uses. That keeps the tenet 1 promise, adds little to the mini-PC's footprint (no new process or service, and the file is already there), and makes the restart behavior in QUEUE-004 concrete. It also removes the awkward asymmetry between the two nodes. The cost is small: a schema, a write on accept and on dispatch, and reloading the queue on startup. If you take this route, the job-queue Decisions table should gain a row recording the choice and the rejected in-memory option.

If you'd rather keep the queue in memory, which tenet 3 supports, the minimum is to record that as an explicit exception. Add a Decisions row that names tenet 1 and the overnight goal as traded away, and states the cost: a restart during the night loses accepted jobs. Then order the tenets in the HLD so a future reader can see that tenet 3 was chosen over tenet 1 here on purpose.

Either way, I'd also make QUEUE-004 say what happens to accepted jobs on restart. Right now the spec describes only the rebuild from printers, and a cold reader can't tell whether loss is intended. The three remedies are your call, so I haven't picked among them beyond the recommendation above.
