**F1 (high): Accepted jobs live only in memory, so an overnight restart loses them.** The job-queue design holds the queue "entirely in process memory." Its Architecture section says jobs accepted but not yet dispatched are "gone" after a restart, and that members resubmit them. The Context says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip," so restarts happen without anyone watching. That is the condition where the HLD's first tenet, "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," gets tested. The goal "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup, with no member present" also fails under that condition. The design pays this cost knowingly, but the tenet-cascade lens (a mechanism that works against a tenet under a condition the docs describe) and the decision-capture lens (a live choice a cold reader would question) both apply, and the Decisions table has no row for queue storage. The registry, by contrast, has a row for its SQLite store. A future reader sees a persisted registry and an in-memory queue and can't tell whether that split was intended. The consequence is concrete: a member uploads a part at 9 pm, a power blip restarts the mini-PC at 2 am, and the member finds no part in the morning and no record of why.

Three ways to resolve it, and the choice is yours:

1. **Change the design.** Persist accepted jobs before acknowledging the upload, so a restart rebuilds the queue the same way it rebuilds printer state. The registry already uses SQLite, so this may be a small addition. The Decisions row for "single SQLite file" says nothing else needs concurrent writers, so that rationale would need a look if the queue writes to the same file.
2. **Keep it in memory and record the exception.** Add a Decisions row to the job-queue LLD that names the promise tenet as the one traded against, says why (simplicity on modest hardware, or the expectation that the mini-PC rarely restarts), and states the member-facing cost. This closes F1's decision-capture finding as well.
3. **Revise the tenet.** If "promise" was meant as a goal and not a guarantee, reword it so it stops implying durability, for example "members should be able to upload and walk away, and be told when a job was lost."

My lean is option 1. The "stays small" tenet and the promise tenet are both in the HLD, and persistence costs little on this hardware. But that's a judgment about your priorities, and option 2 is a defensible answer if you know the restart risk is low in practice. If you go with 1 or 2, the LLD and specs need a matching change: QUEUE-004 currently only covers rebuilding from printers, so a persisted queue would need its own spec.
