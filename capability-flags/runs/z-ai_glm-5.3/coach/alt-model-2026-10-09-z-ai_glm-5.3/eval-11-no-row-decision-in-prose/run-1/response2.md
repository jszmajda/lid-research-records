**The promise the tenet makes, and the mechanism that quietly breaks it.** The HLD's first tenet (docs/high-level-design.md:20) says "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," and the Goals sharpen it: "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." Meanwhile the job-queue design (docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14) states the queue is held *entirely in process memory* and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" — and the LLD's own Context (line 10) names the exact operating condition that triggers it: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." So the documented behavior under the documented condition is: a member uploads in the evening, walks away per the promise, the machine power-blips at midnight, and the job silently vanishes — the exact failure mode the tenet exists to close out. The two never share wording, which is why the conflict isn't obvious on any single read; this is *tenet cascade* — design mechanisms must be checked against the HLD's tenets under the conditions the docs themselves describe, not just against their own local phrasing. What makes it worse rather than merely unrecorded is that it's also a decision-capture gap: there's no row in the Decisions & Alternatives table (lines 29–33) naming what was given up, so a future session reading the LLD cold has no way to know whether in-memory queues are a deliberate tradeoff or an oversight. And the tradeoff isn't even symmetric across the project — the printer-registry LLD (printer-registry-design.md:14) *does* persist to a SQLite file, and uploaded G-code is already kept on disk under a content hash. The queue's tiny metadata (id, class, priority, position) is the single point of loss in a system where everything around it survives the restart.
