**F1 — High. The in-memory queue works against the HLD's core tenet under conditions the docs themselves describe.** `job-queue-design.md` holds the queue "entirely in process memory" and states plainly that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" — while the same doc's Context says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip," and the HLD's first tenet reads "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away." This is the pattern *tenet cascade* is about — each design node should lean the way the HLD's tenets lean, and the conflict is dangerous precisely because the two share no wording: nothing in the queue text mentions the promise, so nothing on the page flags it. It matters most right now, *before* implementation: once tests and code are written against the in-memory design, a test will assert loss-on-restart as correct behavior, and the intent layer will have silently flipped without anyone deciding to. The evening-upload/morning-pickup goal in the HLD is exactly the scenario a power blip breaks.

**F2 — Medium. The durability choice has no record, so every future reader re-derives it cold.** Under *decision capture* (part of *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold*), a choice a cold reader would still question needs its reasoning on the page — and this one invites the question from both directions: the registry persists in SQLite while the member-facing promise doesn't, and the accepted cost ("members resubmit") sits in Architecture prose with no Decisions & Alternatives row naming the rejected alternative. The cost of leaving it unrecorded is that each future session samples its own answer — some will persist the queue, some won't — and the system loses the ability to say which is intent and which is accident. The smallest capture that settles it is a fuller row in the job-queue Decisions table: rejected alternative (durable queue), accepted cost, and why.

**What I'd recommend — your choice among three, not mine to pick.** For a tenet conflict, the remedies are:

1. **Change the design.** Persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs — G-code files are already durably on disk under content hashes, so persisting the queue entries themselves (in the existing SQLite file, or a small sidecar) is cheap and arguably serves tenet 3 ("stays small") too. This is the only remedy that fully honors tenet 1; the walk itself would be an LLD edit plus a new EARS spec (a `QUEUE-006` about restart recovery), done through `/linked-intent-dev` so the cascade is walked properly.
2. **Record the exception.** Keep the in-memory design but add a Decisions row that names the tenet traded against and why — something like: "queue durability → in-memory only; tenet 1 ('a submitted job is a promise') traded against tenet 3 (stays small); accepted cost: a restart during unattended hours loses undispatched jobs." This closes F2 with the same row, and makes the promise's real bounds ("the promise holds while the service runs") explicit rather than discovered.
3. **Revise the tenet.** Restate tenet 1 to match reality — e.g., "the queue survives what the mini-PC survives" or an explicitly best-effort promise — if losing an overnight job to a blip is genuinely acceptable to members.

Note that options 2 and 3 also close F2 with the same row; option 1 replaces it with a new decision record. F7 (the unordered tenets) is the same tension from the HLD side — whichever way you go here, ordering tenet 1 above tenet 3 (or annotating the conflict) would make the lean explicit for the next uncovered decision.

Which way do you want to lean — honor the promise in the design, or narrow it on the record?
