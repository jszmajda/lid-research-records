Here's the detail on those two findings — they're two views of the same design choice, which is why the report split them.

**F2 — High. The in-memory queue works against your first tenet under the exact conditions your own LLD describes.** The job-queue LLD states the queue is "held entirely in process memory" and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" (`docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14`). Its own Context names when that happens: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" — the updates aren't even accidents, they're expected. Now hold that against the HLD's first tenet, "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," and the goal "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." The worst-case timeline writes itself: a member uploads at 9pm, the queue is deep, their job is accepted-but-not-dispatched at 3am when the OS updates — and they arrive in the morning to nothing, with no way to even know the job vanished. Overnight is precisely when the most jobs sit in the loss window, and the docs share no wording between the tenet and the mechanism, which is why the conflict is easy to miss. Under *tenets capture forward-looking preference* — tenets are the tie-breakers a future session leans on when no spec covers it — a mechanism that quietly defeats a tenet under a described operating condition is the conflict that principle exists to catch. The cost of leaving it unresolved isn't just one lost print: the first implementation session will read the tenet and the Architecture section and get opposite signals, and which one wins becomes a coin flip.

**F3 — High. The same choice is also under-captured: it survives only in Architecture prose, with no Decisions & Alternatives row.** The table in `job-queue-design.md:29-32` records scheduling order, job identifiers, and the upload limit — all good rows with rejected alternatives and accepted costs — but durability, arguably the most consequential choice in the doc, has no row. A cold reader would question it immediately: the LLD already keeps every G-code file on disk under a content hash, so persisting the queue alongside them is cheap and the obvious question "why not?" has no recorded answer. Under *docs carry current intent* — docs must let a cold reader reconstruct what was given up and what cost was accepted — a live decision with no row is intent that exists only in the author's head. The reason this compounds: the next session won't know whether in-memory-only was deliberate or just the first draft, and "was this on purpose?" is exactly the question LID is built to make unaskable.

**What I'd recommend — your call between three remedies for F2, and note that either of the first two also closes F3:**

- **Change the design.** Persist the queue (a small SQLite file or JSON beside the content-hash store would do; the registry already chose SQLite for this same mini-PC). Startup then restores accepted-but-not-dispatched jobs, and the existing poll-the-printer rebuild still covers dispatched ones. This keeps tenet 1 intact and matches the member experience the HLD's Problem section promises. If you go this way, the Architecture section changes and the new choice gets its Decisions row.
- **Record the exception.** Keep the in-memory design, and add a Decisions row naming the trade: the rejected alternative (persisting queue state on disk), the accepted cost (accepted jobs lost on restart, members resubmit), and the tenet traded against with the why — e.g., the file store makes resubmission cheap, so the cost is judged tolerable. This makes the drift deliberate and legible instead of silent. It's the smallest move, and it simultaneously resolves F3.
- **Revise the tenet.** Reword tenet 1 to scope the promise honestly (e.g., "a dispatched job is a promise"). Worth weighing carefully, though: the current tenet expresses exactly what the Problem section says members want — upload and walk away — so this remedy moves the project's stated values rather than the design.

One honest observation while you weigh them: even under the current design, nothing in the specs tells a member their job vanished after a restart — QUEUE-004 rebuilds farm state but nothing covers notifying anyone of the loss. Whatever remedy you pick, that silence is part of the cost being either fixed or recorded.
