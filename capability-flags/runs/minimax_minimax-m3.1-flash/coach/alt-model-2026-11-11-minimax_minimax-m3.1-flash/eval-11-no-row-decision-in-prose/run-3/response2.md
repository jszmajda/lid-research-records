**F2 — High. The in-memory queue contradicts the project's headline promise, and no doc records the trade.**

**The observation.** `docs/high-level-design.md:20` makes it a tenet: *"A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away."* The HLD's first goal (`docs/high-level-design.md:26`) is *"Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* But the job-queue LLD states the queue is *"a per-printer-class list of accepted jobs held entirely in process memory"* and that *"jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them"* (`docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14`). The LLD's own Context section supplies the trigger condition in its first paragraph: *"the service runs on the workshop mini-PC, which takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip"* (`job-queue-design.md:10`). So the failure mode isn't an edge case the design survives — it's the normal operating condition of the exact scenario the HLD is selling. QUEUE-004 (`job-queue-specs.md:6`) covers rebuilding farm state by polling printers for the job each one *is running*; nothing covers the accepted-but-not-yet-dispatched jobs, and no Decisions row anywhere names this as a cost the project has accepted.

**The principle.** *Tenets capture forward-looking preference* — a tenet is a tie-breaker stating which way to lean when a decision has two defensible answers and no spec covers it. A mechanism that works against a tenet needs a recorded exception naming which tenet was traded and why. Here the trade was never recorded: the tenet says "promise," the design says "gone," and the doc that holds both never notices. There's a second principle layered on it — *intent leads; code is compiled output* (specs are the source of truth; code is the compiled result) — because "accepted jobs are lost on restart" currently exists **only in LLD prose**. There's no spec line for it in either direction, so the loss isn't pinned as intent, and the next session could read "members resubmit them" as settled fact or as a leftover. It also connects to F4: the behavior is one of the LLD-stated behaviors with no spec at all.

**Why it matters.** This isn't a hypothetical outage — a power blip during the unattended evening window silently discards exactly the jobs the tenet promises to hold. The member's upload is acknowledged, the member walks away, and the job evaporates with no notification and no record. Because the loss is invisible and the system can't tell the member, the member's next action is to find out by asking in chat — the exact behavior the HLD says the service exists to replace. And the failure compounds in a specific way: because the queue is in memory but the registry is in SQLite (`printer-registry-design.md:14`), the farm survives a restart with printers correctly known and jobs correctly gone. The service comes back up looking healthy and silently owes people prints. That's the kind of incoherence that makes a tenet stop being believed — once members learn a restart loses uploads, they stop walking away, and the headline scenario quietly doesn't work anymore even when the machine never blips.

**Related placement finding (F5).** The durability policy isn't really a job-queue decision — it governs *both* siblings, since the registry already persists in SQLite and the queue doesn't. Under *intent attaches at the lowest node that dominates it* (a decision lives at the lowest design node whose subtree contains everything it affects), the durability *rationale* belongs above the two leaves. There is no intermediate node today, and for two leaves you probably don't want to invent one; the natural home is the HLD's Key Design Decisions section — which is precisely the section F7 says is missing. The mechanics (what's stored, in what form) stay in the leaf LLDs. If the durability story later grows — retention, per-job state transitions, migration off SQLite — that's the moment a sub-HLD earns its place under the tree, rather than today.

**Three ways to close it — your call.**

**A. Change the design: persist the accepted queue durably.** SQLite is already a dependency in the sibling node, and the registry's own Decisions row says *"nothing else needs concurrent writers"* — so the mini-PC cost argument has already been paid. Keep the in-memory per-class list as the working view, add a durable record of accepted-but-not-dispatched jobs, and reload it at startup alongside the existing printer polling (the two are already cleanly separated — QUEUE-004's poll recovers *running* jobs, the persisted record recovers *waiting* ones). This also lets you add the spec the segment currently lacks: *"When the service restarts, the system SHALL restore accepted-but-undispatched jobs to the head of their class queue,"* marked `[ ]` as an active gap until code and tests exist.

**B. Record the exception.** Keep the design, and add a Decisions row to the job-queue LLD naming the tenet traded against (*"A submitted job is a promise"*), the condition (restart during the unattended window), the accepted cost (member resubmits, silently), and whatever mitigation you actually intend — a notification, a status endpoint, nothing. This closes F2 and F6-style capture gaps at once, and it's honest. Its cost is that it records a promise the HLD still advertises; a reader of the tenet alone will believe something the design doesn't deliver.

**C. Revise the tenet.** Narrow the promise to what the design actually keeps — something like *"an accepted job survives until dispatch is attempted within the current process lifetime"* — and let the HLD's overnight goal go with it, or soften it to reflect that a job is at risk across a restart. This is the honest option but the most expensive: it moves the goalposts on the project's main value proposition, and given the mini-PC's stated power-blip exposure, you'd probably be writing a smaller promise than you actually want to make.

My recommendation is **A**, with the durable rationale recorded in the HLD's Key Design Decisions and the mechanics captured in the job-queue LLD — the asymmetry between two sibling nodes is what makes the current state hard to reason about, and it's cheap to remove given SQLite is already there. But **B** is a legitimate choice if the in-memory queue is a deliberate simplicity bet you want to keep, and **C** is worth considering if you're not committed to unattended overnight operation yet.
