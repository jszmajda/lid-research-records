**F1 — High. The in-memory queue contradicts the "a submitted job is a promise" tenet, and nothing on the page records the trade-off.**

The queue node states that jobs are "held entirely in process memory," that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" (`docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14`), and its Context notes the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" (`docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:10`) — so restarts are routine, not exceptional. Meanwhile the HLD's first tenet says "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" (`docs/high-level-design.md:20`), and the goal is "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present" (`docs/high-level-design.md:26`). Under exactly the condition these docs describe, an accepted job silently disappears with nobody awake to notice; QUEUE-004 (`docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-specs.md:6`) only recovers jobs that already reached a printer, so the loss falls entirely on the accepted-but-undispatched window — the overnight window the tenet is about. This is a *tenet cascade* finding — *tenets capture forward-looking preference* (tie-breakers for decisions no spec has fixed) — because the mechanism works against a stated tenet under a documented operating condition, and it's also a *decision capture* finding — *live decisions need a recorded row* — because "we accept the loss and make resubmission cheap" is exactly the kind of choice a cold reader would still question and want to reverse, yet it survives only in Architecture prose with no Decisions & Alternatives row. The cost of leaving it as-is is that the tenet reads as the design's promise while the design quietly breaks it — and every future session that works from the HLD's intent will build on a false premise, so the drift compounds rather than resolving.

**Three ways to settle it — your call, since each is defensible**

- **Change the design:** persist accepted jobs (the registry already persists printers in SQLite; the same file could hold the queue, or a small jobs table), and let the service rebuild the queue on startup rather than only the farm state. This makes the tenet literally true, at the cost of a schema and a startup-recovery path to specify — which then need their own LLD detail and EARS lines (accept-persist, restore-on-start, resubmit-idempotency).
- **Record the exception:** keep the in-memory design, but add a Decisions & Alternatives row in the job-queue LLD naming the tenet being traded against ("a submitted job is a promise"), why the trade was accepted (mini-PC disk limits, single-writer simplicity, and the content-hash dedup that makes resubmission cheap), and what members are told when a job is lost. This closes both findings at once — the tenet conflict and the decision-capture gap — with the smallest change.
- **Revise the tenet:** narrow the promise to something the design keeps, e.g. "an accepted job survives dispatch attempts, not restarts," and say plainly in the HLD that accepted jobs are lost on restart. This is the honest version, but it weakens the member-facing promise that the whole evening-upload story rests on.

If it helps, my own read: the exception row is the cheapest move that stops the contradiction from spreading, and the durable queue is worth pricing if the makerspace expects overnight unattended runs — which the HLD's goal line says they do. Whichever way you go, it needs to reach EARS: today no spec states the durability guarantee at all, so there is currently nothing to test against (this overlaps F4's coverage gaps).
