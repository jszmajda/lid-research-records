Here's the detail on the in-memory queue finding — it spans two of the findings from the report, plus a spec-level gap they both leave open.

**F3 — High. The queue's durability trade-off is captured nowhere in the arrow.** `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14` states it plainly: the queue is "a per-printer-class list of accepted jobs held entirely in process memory… jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them," and line 10 tells us why the risk is real — "the mini-PC… takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." That sentence is the most consequential architectural choice in the whole design, and it appears exactly once, in a sentence of prose, with no Decisions row (`job-queue-design.md:29-33` covers scheduling order, job identifiers, and the upload size limit — not the queue store), no rationale, no accepted cost, and no spec anywhere in `job-queue-specs.md`. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — a doc carries today's intent with no dependence on the conversation that produced it — the failure here is specific: a cold reader (a future agent, or a new makerspace volunteer) sees a design that accepts a member's job and says nothing about what happens to that promise. This is also the *decision capture* dimension: a decision that stays live once landed — would a cold reader still question it, or want to reverse it? Yes, immediately: "why is the queue in memory when the registry right next door persists to SQLite, and the whole point is that members upload and walk away?" The cost of leaving it uncaptured compounds: every future session re-derives the same doubt, and the likeliest outcome is a well-meaning agent quietly "fixing" it by adding persistence — which is exactly the kind of drift that the arrow exists to make visible and deliberate.

**F4 — High. The design works against one of the HLD's own tenets, and the exception isn't recorded.** `docs/high-level-design.md:20` states the tenet: "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away." `high-level-design.md:26` restates it as a goal: "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." But under the condition the LLD itself names (unattended OS update, power blip), an accepted job evaporates and the member has to be present to resubmit it — the promise breaks precisely in the scenario the tenet is about. Meanwhile `high-level-design.md:22` holds the opposite-leaning tenet: "The mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small." That's a genuine tension between two tenets, and two things make it worse: the tenets are an unordered list, so the workflow convention "order them so that when two conflict, the higher one wins" can't be applied — there's no statement of which one dominates; and no Decisions row records the trade as a deliberate exception. Under *tenet cascade* — do the design nodes lean the way the HLD's tenets say to lean, and when they don't, is the exception on the record? — this is the clearest case of the conflict the dimension is designed to catch. The cost of leaving it: the arrow claims a promise the design doesn't keep, so a future agent reading only the specs and code would have no idea whether durability was an oversight or a choice, and the HLD's tenet list stops being usable as a tie-breaker for any *other* future choice, because the one time it mattered, the design quietly went the other way.

**Related — the behavior has no spec, in either direction.** Whether you keep the volatile queue or persist it, the current arrow specifies nothing: `job-queue-specs.md` has five IDs and none of them mentions job loss on restart, resubmission, or recovery. That's the *effective intent-tree alignment* dimension — an LLD behavior with no spec is unnamed intent that drifts silently, because there's nothing to test against. It also means the member-facing question is unanswered: the design says "members resubmit them," but nothing specifies whether the member is *told* — that the job was lost, that resubmission is needed, or that the resubmission was cheap because G-code is stored under a content hash (`job-queue-design.md:14`). A member who uploaded at 9pm and collected at 8am needs to learn about the loss from somewhere.

### What I'd recommend doing

The tenet remedy is genuinely your call — the standard three are: **change the design** (persist the queue), **record the exception** (a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why), or **revise the tenet** (scope what "a promise" covers). My read, for what it's worth: this project's own constraints make the third option weaker than it looks — the registry already persists to a single SQLite file (`printer-registry-design.md:14`), so "the service stays small" does less work here than the tenet assumes, and the G-code payloads are already on disk under a content hash, so persisting a few queue rows would add a table, not a subsystem. That said, the honest counter-argument is real: this is a makerspace, not a data center, and the mini-PC's disk is the thing you wanted to protect.

Concretely, and smallest-change-first:

1. **Record the decision either way** — add a row to the `## Decisions & Alternatives` table in `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md` naming the rejected alternative (persist the queue to SQLite alongside the registry), the chosen path, and the accepted cost (accepted jobs lost on restart; members resubmit). If you keep the volatile queue, that row doubles as the tenet-exception record for F4 and closes both findings at once. If you change the design, the row records the new choice and its cost instead.
2. **Give it a spec** — add an ID to `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-specs.md`, e.g. a `QUEUE-006` that states what happens to accepted-but-undispatched jobs at startup, and — if the volatile queue stays — what the member is told on return.
3. **Order the tenets, or note the precedence** — in `docs/high-level-design.md`, make the conflict explicit: either move the promise tenet above the "keeps small" tenet, or narrow the promise tenet so it doesn't claim what the design can't deliver. Whichever way you go, the two should not sit in an unordered list where they silently disagree.
4. **Re-check the markers** — while you're in the specs file, note that all eight specs currently read `[x]` (implemented) even though the repo has no code or tests (F2). If the implementation lives outside this repo, that's worth a note in the instruction file; if it doesn't exist yet, the markers should be `[ ]` so the arrow tells the truth about where the work actually stands.
