These two findings are really one thread, so let me walk them together.

**F2 — High. The in-memory queue quietly breaks the project's central promise.** `job-queue-design.md` states the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and that on restart "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" (lines 14). The same doc's Context names the triggering conditions as routine, not exotic: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" (line 10). Trace that against the HLD's first tenet — *"A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away"* — and its first goal, *"Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* A member uploads at 9pm and leaves; an unattended update reboots the mini-PC at 2am; the accepted-but-undispatched job is gone; nobody is present to resubmit, and the morning pickup is empty. This is the pattern behind LID's *tenets capture forward-looking preference* principle — a tenet is the lean a design should follow when no spec covers the case — applied as a **cascade check**: the two never share wording (the tenet says "promise," the design says "held in memory"), yet under a condition the design itself describes, the mechanism defeats the tenet and the goal. Why it matters: this is exactly the kind of seam that stays invisible until it fires in production, because nothing in the specs or tests contradicts it — the arrow looks coherent while the intent is quietly violated, and the cost lands on the one scenario the whole service exists to deliver.

**F3 — Medium, and coupled to F2. The durability tradeoff was decided but never written down.** Choosing to hold the queue in memory — and to accept losing accepted jobs on restart — is a real design decision, but it lives only as narration in the Architecture section; `job-queue`'s Decisions & Alternatives table (lines 29–33) covers scheduling order, job identifiers, and upload size, and is silent on persistence. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — a future reader should be able to reconstruct a choice from the page without the conversation that produced it — this is an under-captured decision: a cold reader would immediately question "why can we lose a job that we told the member was a promise?" and, finding no row, would either re-litigate it or assume it was an oversight rather than a choice. Capturing it makes the live intent legible and stops the next session from silently "fixing" it the wrong way.

**What I'd recommend** — and here the coach surfaces, you decide; I don't edit the docs:

For **F2**, you have three honest options, and the right one depends on what you actually want the promise to mean:
- **Change the design** so accepted jobs survive a restart — e.g., persist the accepted-but-undispatched queue (the registry already keeps a SQLite file on the same mini-PC per `printer-registry-design.md`, so the durability substrate is already in the house). This makes the mechanism match the tenet.
- **Record the exception** — add a Decisions row in `job-queue-design.md` naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g., "resubmit is cheap because G-code is content-hashed on disk; in-memory keeps the service small per the third tenet"). This keeps the current behavior but makes it a declared choice rather than a latent trap.
- **Revise the tenet** if "upload and walk away" was always meant to tolerate resubmission — though given the overnight-pickup goal, I'd be surprised.

Note that the middle option — recording the exception — also closes **F3**, since the Decisions row *is* the missing capture. If you instead change the design, write the row anyway so the persistence choice (and the alternative you rejected) is on the record. Either way the remedy here is the smallest one that settles it: a fuller Decisions & Alternatives row, not a standalone decision doc — this is a single tradeoff, not a researched multi-option debate that needs its own page.

One thing worth flagging: this decision brushes right up against F1. If you move to a persisted queue, that's new intent that wants a spec (something like "When the service restarts, accepted-but-undispatched jobs SHALL be restored") and then tests and code citing it — so it's a natural moment to start closing the arrow downstream rather than patching prose alone.
