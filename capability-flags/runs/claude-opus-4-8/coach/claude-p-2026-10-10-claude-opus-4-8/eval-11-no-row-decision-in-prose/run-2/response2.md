Here's the detailed walkthrough of the two entangled findings about the in-memory queue.

**F2 — High. The in-memory queue silently drops accepted jobs on restart, and that defeats the project's defining promise.** `job-queue-design.md` states the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and that on startup "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same doc's Context names exactly when this fires: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together on the project's own timeline — a member uploads in the evening and walks away, an unattended OS update or blip restarts the service overnight, and their accepted-but-undispatched job is gone with no one present to resubmit it. That works directly against the HLD tenet *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away"* and the goal *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* This relates to *tenets capture forward-looking preference* (a tenet is the lean a future session should take when no spec covers the call) — here a mechanism quietly contradicts a tenet, and the two share no wording, so nothing in the specs flags the collision. Why it matters: a tenet that the design silently violates is worse than no tenet, because it gives false confidence that the "upload and walk away" case is handled when the single most likely overnight event breaks it; the next session reading the queue design has no signal that this was ever weighed. The remedy is genuinely your choice, and I'd lay out three rather than pick one: **change the design** so accepted jobs survive restart (persist the accepted-but-undispatched queue — the registry already runs a SQLite file on the same box, so the durability substrate exists); **record the exception** with a Decisions & Alternatives row naming the tenet traded against and why losing undispatched jobs is acceptable; or **revise the tenet** if "walk away" was never meant to cover pre-dispatch durability. These live in the core workflow, not here — point `/linked-intent-dev` at whichever you choose and it'll cascade the change down to specs.

**F3 — Medium (and closed by one option above). The durability choice isn't captured as a decision at all — it lives only in Architecture prose.** The `job-queue` Decisions & Alternatives table records scheduling order, job identifiers, and the 64 MB upload limit — each with a rejected alternative and an accepted cost, which is exactly the right form. But the in-memory-vs-persisted-queue choice, which is a bigger call than any of those three, appears only as a narrative sentence in the Architecture section with no row. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* (the keep-side: a decision a cold reader would still question or want to reverse belongs in the Decisions table), this is an under-captured decision: a fresh reader landing on "jobs… are gone, and members resubmit them" will immediately ask "why not persist the queue?" and the doc gives them no answer, so they either re-litigate it or quietly reverse it. Why it matters: terse rows are fine when the choice explains itself, but this one doesn't — the reasoning (presumably: simplicity on modest hardware, members resubmit cheaply via the content-hash store) is load-bearing and currently unwritten. The smallest fix that settles it is a single Decisions row: *Decision* = queue durability; *Chosen* = in-memory, lost on restart; *Rationale* = rejected persisting the queue because [the reason], accepted cost = accepted-but-undispatched jobs lost on restart. A full decision doc would be overkill here — nothing else in the project sizes itself around this choice, so a row is the right resolution. Note that if you take the "record the exception" option under F2, that same row closes this finding too — so you may only have one edit to make, not two.
