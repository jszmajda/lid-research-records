Here's the detail on the in-memory queue thread — it runs through three of the findings (F1, F2, and F5), so I'll take them as one story.

**F1 — High. The job-queue holds accepted jobs only in process memory, and loses undispatched ones on restart — under a condition the LLD itself names.** `job-queue-design.md` is explicit on both halves: the Architecture section says the queue is "held entirely in process memory" and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" — while the Context section, one paragraph up, says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." So the exact event that wipes the queue is a described, expected operating condition, not a remote edge case. Line that up against the HLD's first tenet, *a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away*, and the goal *evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present*: a job accepted at 9pm and still queued when a 2am unattended update reboots the box is silently gone, and the member who was promised they could walk away is the one person not there to resubmit. This is the *tenets capture forward-looking preference* principle — design mechanisms should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean — and it bites precisely because the two never share wording: "a submitted job is a promise" and "jobs… are gone" describe the same moment from opposite sides. Left as-is, the service's headline promise quietly fails on the nights it matters most, and because nothing in the docs flags the tension, a future session reading the LLD will treat volatile queuing as settled intent and build more on top of it. The fix is a design choice, not a doc edit — see the recommendations below.

**F2 — High. That durability trade-off never appears in job-queue's Decisions & Alternatives.** The table captures three other decisions well — FIFO-vs-shortest-job-first, slug-vs-UUID, the 64 MB limit — each naming its rejected alternative and its accepted cost. But the choice to keep the queue in memory (and the asymmetry with printer-registry, which persists its table to SQLite in `printer-registry-design.md`) is stated only as a bare architecture sentence, with no row explaining what was given up or why. Under *docs carry current intent* — a doc should let a cold reader reconstruct the live decisions without the conversation that produced them — this is an under-captured decision: a reader who notices that the registry is durable but the members' promised jobs are not would immediately question it and want to reverse it, and the docs give them nothing to reconstruct the reasoning from. The cost is that the single most consequential architectural decision in this node is also its least explained, so every future discussion about queue reliability starts from scratch. (F1 and F2 are the same choice seen two ways — the design concern and the capture gap are separate findings on purpose: the fact that the choice looks questionable is what proves the decision is still live and worth recording.)

**F5 — Medium. The HLD's tenets are an unordered list, but two of them pull against each other here.** *A submitted job is a promise* (which wants durability) and *the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small* (which is the likely motivation for the simple in-memory queue) are in direct tension at exactly the point F1 identifies. The *tenets capture forward-looking preference* principle notes that when more than two tenets can conflict, an ordered list is what tells a future session which way to lean — an unordered one leaves the tie-breaker itself a coin-flip. Ordering these (does the promise outrank staying small, or vice versa?) would have pre-decided F1 instead of leaving it to be rediscovered.

**What I'd recommend**

For F1, the tenet/goal conflict, the remedies are yours to choose — I won't pick one for you:

- **Change the design** so accepted-but-undispatched jobs survive a restart — e.g. persist the queue to the same SQLite file the registry already uses, or write accepted jobs to disk alongside the content-hashed G-code that's already being stored. This is the option that actually keeps the promise.
- **Record the exception** — add a Decisions & Alternatives row in `job-queue-design.md` stating that volatile queuing is a deliberate trade against *a submitted job is a promise*, naming why (presumably simplicity on modest hardware) and the accepted cost (lost jobs on restart, members resubmit).
- **Revise the tenet** if "walk away" was never meant to hold across reboots — though given the overnight-unattended goal, that seems the least likely fit.

The key point: **recording the exception also closes F2** — a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and the accepted cost is exactly the capture F2 is asking for. So if you take the "record it" path for F1, F2 resolves in the same stroke. If you take the "change the design" path, you'll still want a short row documenting the new durable approach and why, which closes F2 that way instead. Either way F2 doesn't need separate handling.

For F5, ordering the three tenets in the HLD is a small edit that makes the F1 call reusable for the next decision like it.
