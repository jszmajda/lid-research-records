Here's the detailed walk-through of that finding — and its twin, since they're the same design choice seen from two angles.

**F1 — High. The job-queue keeps accepted jobs only in process memory, which collides with the farm's own operating conditions and the "a submitted job is a promise" tenet.** The Architecture section of `job-queue-design.md` says the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The Context section, a few lines up, states the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" — so restarts aren't an edge case the design can wave off; they're a named, expected condition. Put the two together and the failure is concrete: a member uploads at 9pm and walks away, the mini-PC takes an unattended OS update at 2am, and in the morning there's no print and no notice — exactly the hovering-and-babysitting problem the HLD set out to kill. This runs against the tenet *a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away* (the HLD's forward-looking lean toward treating a submitted job as durable). The reason this is worth catching now rather than later: the tenet and the mechanism share no vocabulary — "promise" / "walk away" never meets "in process memory" / "resubmit" — so nothing in a grep or a casual reread will connect them, and a future session extending the queue will faithfully preserve the in-memory design without ever noticing it quietly defeats the project's headline promise. Tenet-to-leaf conflicts that share no wording are precisely the ones that sit undetected until a member loses an overnight job in the real world.

**F4 — Medium. That same persistence choice has no Decisions & Alternatives row.** The in-memory decision — and its accepted cost, "members resubmit" — lives only in Architecture prose. The job-queue node already has a strong decisions table (FIFO vs. shortest-job-first, slugs vs. UUIDs, both naming the rejected alternative and the cost accepted), which is what makes the omission stand out: persistence is a more load-bearing choice than either of those, yet it's the one not on the record. Under *docs carry current intent* — a doc states the live decision so a cold reader can see what was chosen and what was given up, rather than inferring it — a decision that lives only in body prose reads as a settled fact rather than a weighed trade-off. The consequence is that a future session sees "in memory" as simply how the queue works, not as a choice with a known downside someone accepted, and so never revisits it even when durability becomes the obvious fix.

**What I'd recommend.** These are two findings but one decision, so recording the exception resolves F4 *and* is one of the legitimate ways to resolve F1 — I'd put the choice in front of you rather than pick for you. Three paths, your call:

- **Change the design** — give the queue a durable store so accepted jobs survive a restart. The printer-registry node already runs a single SQLite file on this same mini-PC and explicitly reasons that "nothing else needs concurrent writers"; a persisted queue would make that no longer true, so this path is a real design change with a cross-node implication, not a one-line edit. If you want to go this way, drive it through `/linked-intent-dev` with a description of the durability behavior you want — the workflow will carry it HLD-down into the LLDs, new EARS specs (recovery of accepted-but-undispatched jobs is currently unspecced), tests, and code.
- **Record the exception** — add a Decisions & Alternatives row to `job-queue-design.md`: decision "queue persistence," chosen "in-memory, accepted jobs lost on restart," rationale naming the tenet traded against ("a submitted job is a promise") and why the trade is acceptable (e.g., restarts are rare, resubmit from the content-hashed file on disk is cheap). This keeps the current behavior but makes the trade explicit and closes F4.
- **Revise the tenet** — if walk-away durability isn't actually a promise you intend to keep on this hardware, soften the tenet so the design and the stated intent agree.

A couple of things I'd flag about the options. The first path is the only one that actually delivers the promise; the second and third make the docs honest about *not* delivering it — which is a legitimate choice, but worth making with eyes open given it's the HLD's lead tenet. And whichever you pick, the config-level capture (the Decisions row) is something you'd edit directly or route through `/update-lid` — I don't make the edits myself; the coach stays advisory so this decision, of all of them, gets your review rather than my guess.
