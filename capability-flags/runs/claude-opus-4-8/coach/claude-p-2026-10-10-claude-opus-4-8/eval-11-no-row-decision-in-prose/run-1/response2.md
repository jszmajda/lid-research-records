**F1 — High. The job-queue keeps accepted jobs only in process memory, and the LLD's own context describes exactly when that loses them.** `job-queue-design.md:14` says the queue is "held entirely in process memory" and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" — while `:10` notes the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those two sentences next to the HLD and they collide: a member uploads in the evening and walks away (the tenet *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away"*), the mini-PC reboots overnight, and the undispatched job is silently dropped — at the exact moment the goal *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present"* guarantees nobody is there to resubmit. This is the pattern dimension 19 watches for under *keep the arrow coherent* — a leaf mechanism should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals lean, and here the storage choice works directly against both, under an operating condition the LLD itself names rather than some exotic edge case. Why it matters: the conflict is invisible at the spec level (none of QUEUE-001–005 mention persistence), so a future session implementing the queue will faithfully build the volatile version, and the promise quietly fails in production on the first overnight power blip — the kind of drift that's much cheaper to catch now, in the design, than after members have lost prints.

**F4 — Medium (same choice, capture side).** The volatility is a real, contestable decision, but `job-queue-design.md`'s Decisions & Alternatives table (`:27`) has no row for it — it covers scheduling order, job identifiers, and upload size, but not "queue persistence: in-memory, lost on restart." Under *docs carry current intent* — a decision a cold reader would still question belongs on the page, not just in the author's head — a reader who hits the walk-away promise is left to wonder whether the volatility is a deliberate trade or an oversight.

**What I'd recommend — your call among three, I won't pick for you:**

- **Change the design.** If the promise is load-bearing (and the tenet says it is), persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs — you already store the G-code on disk under a content hash (`:14`), so durably recording the queue entries (slug, class, priority, timestamp) in a small on-disk store — even the same SQLite file the registry already uses — would make restart-survival nearly free and let startup rebuild from disk rather than only from polling live printers (QUEUE-004). That likely wants a new EARS spec too ("When the service restarts, the system SHALL restore accepted-but-undispatched jobs").
- **Record the exception.** If volatility is a deliberate trade (simplicity on modest hardware, restarts assumed rare), add a Decisions & Alternatives row naming the tenet traded against and the accepted cost — e.g. *"Queue persistence · in-memory, lost on restart · rejected on-disk queue to keep the service small; accepted cost: jobs undispatched at a restart are lost and must be resubmitted, in tension with the walk-away promise."* Recording it this way also closes F4, since the decision is then captured.
- **Revise the tenet.** If "upload and walk away" is actually aspirational rather than a hard promise, soften the tenet so the design and the stated intent agree.
