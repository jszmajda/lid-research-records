Here's the detail on the two findings — they're really two views of the same choice, so I'll walk them together and then lay out the options.

**F1 — High. The job-queue holds accepted jobs only in process memory, and the one window where that bites is the window your goal is built around.** `job-queue-design.md:14` says the queue is "held entirely in process memory" and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same doc's Context (`:10`) names the two things that cause restarts — "unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" — and the dispatch model (`:16`) is event-driven: a job sits accepted-but-undispatched until a printer of its class frees up. Stack those together and the failure is concrete: a member uploads at 9pm for an overnight slot, the mini-PC takes an unattended update at 2am, and the job is silently gone by the morning pickup. That runs against the HLD tenet *a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away* and the goal *evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present*. This is the *tenets capture forward-looking preference / keep the arrow coherent* lens — the idea that a leaf mechanism shouldn't defeat one of the project's own tenets under a condition the docs themselves describe. Why it matters here: the conflict is invisible precisely because the tenet and the mechanism share no words ("promise" never appears near "in process memory"), so a future session reading the job-queue leaf in isolation sees a reasonable in-memory design and never feels the tension — the drift sits unattended until a member loses an overnight print and asks why the system broke a promise it never actually made.

**F2 — High. That same volatility choice has no row in the Decisions & Alternatives table.** The table at `job-queue-design.md:27` captures scheduling order, job identifiers, and the upload limit — each naming the rejected alternative *and* the accepted cost, which is exactly the habit you want — but the decision to keep the queue in memory and have members resubmit after a restart isn't there. This is the *docs carry current intent* lens on the keep-side (a live, still-questionable choice belongs on the page as a decision, not buried in Architecture prose). Why it matters: a cold reader hitting the goal and then the in-memory design will immediately ask "did they weigh durability and reject it, or did nobody notice?" Right now the doc can't answer, so every future session re-opens the question from scratch — and because it reads as an oversight rather than a decision, someone will eventually "fix" it by bolting on persistence without knowing whether the modest-hardware tenet was the reason it was left out.

**What I'd recommend** — these are your call, not mine; the coach surfaces, you decide:

For the tenet/goal conflict (F1), there are three honest resolutions and I wouldn't pick for you:
- **Change the design** — persist accepted jobs (even a tiny append-only file or a row in the registry's existing SQLite) so a restart replays the pending queue instead of dropping it. This is the only option that actually makes the promise true, but it spends something against *the service stays small*.
- **Record the exception** — if you consciously accept the loss (resubmitting a cheap, content-hashed file is minor; overnight restarts are rare), write it down as a deliberate trade, naming the tenet you're trading against and why.
- **Revise the tenet or goal** — if "upload and walk away" was always meant to tolerate the occasional resubmit, soften the promise so the docs and the behavior agree.

For the decision-capture gap (F2), the smallest fix that settles it is a single new Decisions & Alternatives row — something like *Queue durability | In-memory, resubmit after restart | Persistence (SQLite/WAL) rejected to keep the service small on modest hardware; accepted cost: jobs accepted-but-undispatched are lost across a restart and members resubmit*. Note that if you choose "record the exception" for F1, that **same row closes F2 as well** — one row resolves both findings.
