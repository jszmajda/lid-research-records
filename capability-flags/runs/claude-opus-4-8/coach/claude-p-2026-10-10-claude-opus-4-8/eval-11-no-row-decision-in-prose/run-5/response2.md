Here's the detailed walk-through of the two linked findings about the in-memory queue.

**F2 — High. The queue holds accepted jobs only in process memory, and your own design says a restart drops the undispatched ones.** job-queue-design.md puts it plainly: the queue is "a per-printer-class list of accepted jobs held entirely in process memory," and "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same doc's Context names exactly when that happens — the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together with the member's story and the gap is concrete: someone uploads at 9pm and walks away, the mini-PC reboots for an overnight update at 2am, and by morning there's no print and no job. That runs against the HLD's lead tenet, *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away,"* and against the goal *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* The relevant principle is *tenets capture forward-looking preference* — a tenet is the lean a future session should take when no spec covers the call, so a design that quietly works against the project's headline tenet is the exact drift the tenet exists to prevent. Why it matters here: the tenet is doing no work if the first component silently overrides it, and the next person who extends the queue will read "held entirely in process memory" as settled intent rather than as a known compromise against the promise. Nothing on the page tells them a trade was made.

**F3 — High. The in-memory choice, and the lost-jobs cost it carries, live in prose but not in job-queue's Decisions & Alternatives table.** That table already does good work — the FIFO row and the slug row both name the rejected alternative and the accepted cost. But the durability decision, which is the one most in tension with the HLD, isn't there; it's only narrated in the Architecture section. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — a doc should let a fresh reader reconstruct what was given up without the original conversation — this is a decision a cold reader would stop and question ("why would a promise-keeping queue lose jobs on reboot?"), which is the test for when a choice needs its reasoning captured. Leaving it in prose means the trade reads as an incidental implementation note rather than a deliberate call, and the alternative that was passed over (persisting the accepted-but-undispatched queue) is invisible.

**What I'd recommend.** These two resolve together, and I'd treat F2 as the real decision and F3 as making whatever you decide legible. For the tenet conflict, the choice is yours — I won't pick for you — but it's one of three:

- **Change the design** so accepted jobs survive a restart. You already persist a durable store for the registry (the SQLite file), and G-code is already on disk under a content hash; persisting the accepted-queue slugs there would close the gap with little new surface, which also respects the "stays small" tenet.
- **Record the exception** — add a Decisions & Alternatives row in job-queue-design.md naming the tenet traded against ("a submitted job is a promise"), the rejected alternative (persisting the queue), and why the loss is acceptable (e.g., restarts are rare, resubmission is cheap because files are content-hashed). If you take this path, it also closes F3 — the row is exactly the capture that finding is asking for.
- **Revise the tenet** if "upload and walk away" isn't actually a hard promise — but given it's the lead tenet and a stated goal, I'd be surprised.

If you keep the current design, the row in option two is the minimum either way — that's the fix for F3 on its own, independent of what you decide about F2. This is advisory, so I won't edit the docs myself; when you've decided, `/update-lid` is the path if the decision touches configuration, or you can edit job-queue-design.md directly. And since F5 (the unordered tenets) is the same "small vs. promise" tension surfacing at the HLD level, whichever way you resolve this is worth reflecting there too — ordering those two tenets states which one wins the next time they pull apart.
