Here's the detail on the two in-memory-queue findings — they're really two angles on the same design choice, so I'll take them together.

**F1 — High. The in-memory queue drops accepted jobs on restart, against the project's headline promise.** The job-queue design states the queue "is a per-printer-class list of accepted jobs held entirely in process memory," and then names the exact failure itself: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip," and "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Set that beside the HLD's first tenet — *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away"* — and against the goal of *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* The concrete collision: a member uploads at 9pm, walks away, an unattended OS update reboots the mini-PC at 2am, and their accepted-but-undispatched job is silently gone — no print, no notice, and the member isn't present to resubmit. This is the pattern dimension 19 (*tenets capture forward-looking preference; keep the arrow coherent* — a leaf mechanism shouldn't defeat an HLD tenet or goal under a condition the docs themselves describe) exists to catch, and it's a sharp instance because the tenet and the mechanism share no wording, so nothing flags the conflict unless you hold both in mind at once. Why it matters: a tenet is the lean a future session reaches for when no spec covers the case. If the design quietly contradicts the tenet and nothing records that it's deliberate, the next person to touch dispatch inherits a system that behaves opposite to its stated promise and can't tell whether that's intent or an accident — exactly the drift that compounds across sessions.

**F2 — High. That persistence choice has no Decisions & Alternatives row.** The job-queue's Decisions table records scheduling order, job identifiers, and the upload size limit — but not the most consequential and most surprising choice in the node: hold the queue in memory and accept losing accepted jobs on restart. Under *docs carry current intent* (the decision-capture keep-side — a choice a cold reader would still question or want to reverse should record what was given up and the cost accepted), this is an under-captured decision: a fresh reader, holding the "submitted job is a promise" tenet, lands on the in-memory architecture and immediately asks "why isn't this persisted, and was the job-loss window really intended?" — with nothing on the page to answer. Why it matters: absent a row, every future session re-opens this question from scratch, and because the choice reads as risky, someone eventually "fixes" it by adding persistence without knowing whether the in-memory design was a deliberate trade (mini-PC simplicity, the "service stays small" tenet) or an oversight. The row is what makes the choice legible as intent rather than accident.

**What I'd recommend**

These two findings have a nice property: one action can close both. For the **tenet conflict (F1)**, the remedy is yours to pick — I won't choose for you — but it's one of three:
- **Change the design** so accepted jobs survive a restart (e.g., persist the accepted-but-undispatched queue to the same SQLite file the registry already uses, or to the on-disk content-hash store that already exists), bringing the mechanism back in line with the promise.
- **Record the exception** — add a Decisions & Alternatives row naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g., the job-loss window is accepted in exchange for staying small on modest hardware, bounded by how rarely the mini-PC reboots).
- **Revise the tenet** if "upload and walk away" is actually aspirational and the real intent tolerates occasional resubmission.

For the **decision-capture gap (F2)**, the remedy is the smallest capture that settles it: a fuller Decisions row for "queue persistence" naming the rejected alternative (persisted queue) and the accepted cost (jobs lost on unplanned restart). Note that the middle option above — recording the exception — *is* that row, so if you go that route it closes F2 at the same time. If you change the design instead, F2 still wants a short row explaining why you moved to persistence, so the next reader doesn't wonder whether in-memory was ever considered.

I'd lean toward treating F1 as a real design question first — the power-blip-at-2am scenario looks like it genuinely defeats the overnight-print goal, not just a cost against it — and letting that answer decide which remedy you reach for.
