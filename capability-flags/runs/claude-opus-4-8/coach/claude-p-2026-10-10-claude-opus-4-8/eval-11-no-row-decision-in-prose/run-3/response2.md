Happy to. These two are really one issue seen from two angles — the design choice itself (F1) and the fact that it isn't written down (F2). Here they are in detail, then what I'd do about it.

**F1 — High. The in-memory queue works against the project's own "promise" tenet.** `job-queue-design.md` says the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and on startup the service "rebuilds its picture of the farm by polling each registered printer for the job it is currently running; jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same doc's Context notes the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together and trace the overnight path the product is built for: a member uploads in the evening and walks away; a job sits queued-but-not-yet-dispatched; the mini-PC reboots for an update or a blip; the queued job is silently gone; the member arrives in the morning to no print and no notice. That cuts directly against the HLD tenet *a submitted job is a promise — members should be able to upload and walk away* and the goal *evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup, with no member present*. This is the *tenet cascade* principle — leaf mechanisms should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean; the signal it's a real conflict and not just a cost is that the mechanism and the tenet share no wording ("held in process memory" vs. "a promise"), so it's exactly the kind of drift that slips through unless you walk the operating condition. Why it matters here: the window is small (only the accepted-but-undispatched slice is at risk, and the G-code file itself survives on disk under its content hash), but it opens precisely during unattended overnight hours — the one scenario the whole service exists to serve. And "members resubmit them" is the tell: resubmission requires the member to be present, which is the thing the promise says they shouldn't have to be.

**F2 — High. That trade-off isn't in the Decisions & Alternatives table.** The `job-queue` decisions table captures scheduling order, job identifiers, and the upload-size limit — each naming a rejected alternative and an accepted cost — but there's no row for the choice to keep the queue in volatile memory. A cold reader (the next session, with no memory of this conversation) meets "held entirely in process memory" as a bare fact, with nothing saying durability was considered and deliberately traded for simplicity, or under what reasoning. This is *docs carry current intent* — a live decision should be captured where a reader can see what was given up. Why it matters: right now a future agent can't tell whether the volatility is a deliberate "keep it small" call or an accident nobody weighed, so it can't safely either defend it or change it. Given F1, that ambiguity is where the promise quietly breaks.

**What I'd recommend**

Since these are genuinely coupled, the first move is to *decide*, then record — and the recording step closes F2 no matter which way F1 goes. For the tenet conflict, you have three legitimate exits and I'd let the product call drive it, not the methodology:

- **Change the design** so the queue survives restart. The cheapest version fits the existing architecture surprisingly well: you already persist G-code on disk under a content hash and already have a SQLite file next door in `printer-registry`. Persisting the accepted-but-undispatched queue entries (slug, class, priority, content hash, enqueue time) to a small durable store and replaying them on startup — alongside the existing printer-polling rebuild in QUEUE-004 — would honor the promise without really growing the system. This is the option I lean toward, because it resolves the conflict at its root rather than documenting around it, and it stays within the "service stays small" tenet.
- **Record the exception** — if you decide the volatility is acceptable (e.g., the realistic restart window is tiny and a resubmit nudge is good enough). Add a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why, and I'd pair it with a spec for the member-facing behavior on loss (notify on restart, so the promise degrades loudly instead of silently). This is the smallest honest capture.
- **Revise the tenet** — if, on reflection, "upload and walk away" was always meant with a "best-effort across restarts" caveat. Less likely here given how central the overnight goal is, but it's a valid resolution if the promise was overstated.

Whichever you pick, the durability decision wants a Decisions & Alternatives row — that single row is the entire fix for F2, and if you take the "record the exception" path it's also the fix for F1. I'd stop short of a standalone decision doc; this is a one-row call, not a researched option space that a future reader needs to re-run.
