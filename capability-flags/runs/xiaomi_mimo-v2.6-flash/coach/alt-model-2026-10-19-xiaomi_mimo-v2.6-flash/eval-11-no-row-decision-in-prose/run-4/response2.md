**F1 — High. The queue’s in-memory design breaks the project’s own headline tenet, and the trade was never recorded.** The HLD’s first tenet is “A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away,” and the Goals double down: evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup *with no member present*. The job-queue LLD then states that accepted jobs live only in process memory and that “jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them” (`docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md`, Architecture) — under an operating condition the same LLD describes as routine: the mini-PC “takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip” (Context). Hold a tenet and a mechanism in mind and ask whether the mechanism defeats the tenet under a described condition: a member who uploaded at 9 p.m. and went to bed is exactly the person who will *not* be there to resubmit after a 3 a.m. reboot. This is a *tenet cascade* problem (design nodes should lean the way the HLD’s tenets say to lean) — the two docs never use the same words, which is why the conflict sits unnoticed until someone reads them side by side.

It’s also a *decision capture* problem, and the two findings reinforce rather than duplicate each other. In-memory-with-loss-on-restart is a live choice a cold reader would absolutely question or want to reverse — arguably the first question anyone asks when they see the Context paragraph. What they *can’t* reconstruct from the docs is what was given up and why: the Decisions & Alternatives table has rows for scheduling order, job IDs, and upload size, but nothing for queue persistence. Presumably durability was traded for simplicity on modest hardware (the third tenet), but that reasoning lives only in whatever conversation produced the Architecture paragraph. The mechanism is stated as fact; the trade against the promise tenet is nowhere on the page.

**What I’d recommend** — you have three coherent options, and picking one is a design decision, not a coaching one:

1. **Record the exception as-is.** Add a Decisions row: chosen = in-process memory, no persistence of undispatched jobs; rejected alternative = a small persisted queue (e.g., a table in the registry’s existing SQLite file); accepted cost = restart loses undispatched jobs and breaks the promise tenet for that window, mitigated by… whatever you actually want the mitigation to be (member notification? a “pending jobs were lost” surface?). This is the smallest capture and closes the decision-capture half immediately. It leaves the tension visible, which is honest — some teams prefer a documented cost over an unplanned persistence layer.

2. **Fix the design so the tenet holds.** Persist accepted jobs (the LLD already puts G-code on disk under a content hash; persisting the queue row alongside it is the natural extension), then rebuild the queue on startup the same way the LLD already rebuilds farm state by polling printers. This makes the mechanism serve the tenet and needs only a normal LLD edit cascading into any specs you add for restart recovery.

3. **Weaken the tenet** to match the hardware reality (“a submitted job is a promise *once dispatched*” or similar). Cheapest, but it moves the project’s public promise — worth doing only if the makerspace members would actually accept it.

Whichever you pick, QUEUE-004 (“on startup, rebuild farm state by polling printers”) is adjacent and should be re-read in that light: it restores *printer* state, not the *queue*, and right now nothing on the page tells a cold reader which half was deliberate.

Since this landed as both a tenet conflict and an under-captured decision, note that recording the exception in a Decisions row (option 1, or the exception half of option 2) also closes the decision-capture finding — you don’t need two artifacts unless you want a full decision doc on the option space, which for this choice I wouldn’t reach for yet; the row is enough unless other nodes start sizing themselves around the restart window.
