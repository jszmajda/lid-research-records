**F1 — High. The queue holds accepted jobs only in process memory, and nothing records the trade against your own tenets.** `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14` states that the per-class queues live entirely in memory, that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" — while the same file's Context (`:10`) tells us the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." That is the operating condition, stated by your own docs, under which the mechanism collides with two HLD statements: the tenet "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" (`high-level-design.md:20`) and the goal "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present" (`:26`). This is *tenet cascade* — design mechanisms shouldn't quietly defeat a tenet or goal under conditions the docs themselves describe, without an exception on the record — compounded by *decision capture* — a choice a cold reader would immediately question, surviving only as an unstated given in prose. The cost of leaving it as-is is real: the Decisions table right below records three smaller calls (scheduling order, ID slugs, upload cap) each with its rejected alternative and accepted cost, but the largest architectural call in the node has no row at all, so the next session implementing persistence either rediscovers the debate or — worse — "fixes" the design toward durability and silently invalidates the small-service shape the rest of the node assumes. There's also an open edge hiding inside "members resubmit them": nothing says how a member learns their job vanished, which is precisely the member-presence the goal rules out.

The remedy is your call, and I'd offer the three standard options without picking: **change the design** (persist the queue — e.g., the same SQLite the registry already uses — so accepted jobs survive restarts), **record the exception** (add a Decisions row to `job-queue-design.md` naming the persistence choice, the promise tenet traded against it, the rejected durable-queue alternative, and the accepted cost — members may occasionally resubmit), or **revise the tenet** if "upload and walk away" was never meant to survive a power blip. Recording the exception is the smallest capture that settles the question, and since this finding was reported as both a tenet conflict and an under-captured decision, one well-formed row closes both. A decision doc isn't warranted here — no other design node restates or sizes itself around the persistence reasoning.

**F6 — Medium. The tenets can collide and don't say who wins.** "A submitted job is a promise" and "the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small" are both legitimate tie-breakers with defensible opposites — which is exactly why they need an order: F1 is the proof they conflict in practice, and a future session choosing between durability and simplicity currently samples from its own distribution. Under *tenets capture forward-looking preference* (a tenet pre-narrow's the next agent's choice when no spec covers the case), an unordered list where two tenets already pulled in different directions leaves that pre-narrowing half-done. You could resolve it either by stating precedence in the `## Tenets` section (e.g., durability wins when they collide, or smallness wins and the promise is explicitly scoped) or by deciding that F1's recorded exception is enough of a precedent — but pick one deliberately rather than leaving the next reader to arbitrate.

If you decide on the design-change route, that's a walk through `/linked-intent-dev` (new LLD content → specs → tests before code) rather than a doc edit; the row and tenet edits you can apply directly in `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md` and `docs/high-level-design.md`.
