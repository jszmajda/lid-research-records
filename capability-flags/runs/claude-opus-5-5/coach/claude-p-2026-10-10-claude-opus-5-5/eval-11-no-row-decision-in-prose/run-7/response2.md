Two of the report's findings are about this one choice, F1 and F2, and one remedy can close both.

**F1 — High. The in-memory queue works against the project's first tenet and first goal.** `job-queue-design.md` line 14 says the queue is "held entirely in process memory", and that jobs accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart "are gone, and members resubmit them". Line 10 of the same doc says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip". Put together: a member uploads at 9pm, the machine updates at 2am, and the job no longer exists. The HLD's first tenet is "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away", and its first goal is "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present". Resubmitting requires the member to be present, or at least watching, and nothing in the design tells them the job was lost. This is *tenet cascade* — leaf designs should lean the way the HLD's tenets say. It matters because tenets are what a future session falls back on when no spec covers a case. An agent extending the queue will read "a job is a promise" in the HLD and "jobs are gone" in the LLD and have to guess which one is the intent.

**F2 — High. The choice has no row in Decisions & Alternatives.** The table at lines 29–33 covers scheduling order, job identifiers and upload size, but not where the queue lives. A cold reader would question this one straight away, because the registry next door already persists to a SQLite file and the G-code files are already kept on disk. They cannot tell whether in-memory was chosen for "the service stays small", for simplicity, or by default. This is *decision capture* — a choice a reader would want to reverse needs its trade-off on the page. Without it, the next session either "fixes" the design without knowing what it is undoing, or preserves it without knowing why.

**What to do.** There are three ways to resolve F1. The choice is yours, because it depends on which tenet you actually hold more strongly, and the docs don't say.

- **Change the design.** Persist accepted jobs so they survive a restart. The lightest version reuses what is already there: the registry's SQLite file and the content-hashed G-code on disk. This honors the tenet as written. It costs a small amount of extra state on the mini-PC and a new startup step that reloads the queue alongside the printer poll in `QUEUE-004`.
- **Record the exception.** Keep the in-memory queue and add a Decisions row that names the tenet being traded against and why. A row like "Queue storage | In process memory | Persistence rejected because …; accepted cost: a restart drops undispatched jobs, against 'a job is a promise'" would do. This also closes F2. It leaves the overnight goal unmet on any night the machine restarts, so you would likely want a member-facing mitigation, such as notifying members whose jobs were dropped.
- **Revise the tenet.** If the real intent is "best effort, resubmit if the box restarts", soften the tenet and the "no member present" goal in the HLD to say so. This is the honest option if smallness truly outranks reliability, but it changes what the project promises its 120 members.

Whichever you pick, three follow-ons apply:

- **Add the row.** Even if you change the design, "Queue storage" deserves a row, so F2 is closed either way. A row is enough; this does not need a separate decision doc.
- **Spec the restart behavior.** Today no spec says what happens to undispatched jobs on restart; `QUEUE-004` covers only the printer poll. The chosen behavior, whether jobs survive or members are told, should become a `QUEUE-*` spec.
- **Order the tenets.** F7 is the root of this: "a job is a promise" and "the service stays small" pull against each other here, and the list is unordered. Ranking them in the HLD would settle this case and the next one like it.
