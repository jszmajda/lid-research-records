These are the three findings that touch the in-memory queue: F1 and F2 from the high tier, and F8, which is F1's visible edge.

**F1 — High. The in-memory queue pulls against the project's lead tenet under conditions the design itself describes.** `job-queue-design.md` says the queue is "held entirely in process memory" and that jobs accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart "are gone, and members resubmit them" (line 14). The same doc's Context says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" (line 10) and that members upload "mostly in the evening" for overnight dispatch. Put together, a 2 a.m. update reboot drops every waiting job, and the members who submitted them are at home asleep. The HLD's first tenet is "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away", and its first goal is "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present". This is a *tenet cascade* question — leaf designs should lean the way the HLD's tenets say, and where they don't, the exception should be deliberate and visible. It matters because the tenets are what a future session uses to fill gaps no spec covers. An agent extending the queue will read "a job is a promise" in the HLD and "jobs are gone" in the LLD and have to guess which one is the real intent, and whichever way it guesses, half the docs disagree with the code it writes. The third tenet ("the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small") may well be what motivated the choice, but nothing on the page says so.

**F2 — High. The choice has no Decisions & Alternatives row, so a cold reader can't tell whether it was deliberate.** The job-queue's table records scheduling order, job identifiers and upload size, each with a rejected alternative and an accepted cost, which is exactly the shape that makes a decision legible. The in-memory queue is the one choice a reader would actually push back on, and it appears only in Architecture prose. The pushback is easy to predict: G-code is already written to disk under a content hash, and the sibling registry already keeps a SQLite file on the same machine, so persisting the queue looks nearly free. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — a doc should stand on its own without the conversation that produced it — a live decision needs its reasoning on the page. Without it, the likeliest outcome is that someone "fixes" it in a later session without knowing what they are undoing, or leaves it alone assuming a reason exists that doesn't.

**F8 — Medium. The recovery path depends on members knowing their job was lost, and nothing says they are told.** QUEUE-004 covers restart as "rebuild farm state by polling each registered printer for its current job", which captures the printers' side and is silent on the dropped jobs. The LLD's "members resubmit them" assumes a notification, a visible gap in the queue view, or some other signal that isn't designed. Under *coherence is adjacency* — each level of the arrow should carry the whole behaviour of the level above — the spec currently describes half of what the LLD says happens at startup. As written, a member finds out in the morning when there is no part on the bed.

## What to do

F1 is a tenet conflict, so the remedy is your call. There are three legitimate ways out, and they are genuinely different intents rather than better and worse versions of one:

1. **Change the design.** Persist accepted jobs so they survive a restart. The lightest route the existing docs suggest is the SQLite file the registry already owns, or a queue file beside the G-code store. Note that this crosses a node boundary if the queue writes into the registry's store, so it would need to be decided at the HLD or as an explicit dependency, not slipped into the job-queue leaf. QUEUE-004 would then grow to cover restoring the queue, and F8 mostly dissolves.
2. **Record the exception.** Keep the in-memory queue and add a Decisions row to `job-queue-design.md` naming the tenet traded against ("a submitted job is a promise"), the tenet it was traded for (presumably "the service stays small"), and the accepted cost (jobs lost on restart). This also closes F2 in the same edit. F8 stays open and becomes more important: if loss is accepted, the design needs to say how members learn of it, with a spec to match.
3. **Revise the tenet.** If the real intent is "best-effort overnight queue", soften the tenet and the first goal in `docs/high-level-design.md` so they say that. This is the biggest change in meaning, since it also changes what every other design decision is measured against.

Two things hold whichever you choose:

- **Add the Decisions row regardless.** Even under option 1, a row saying "persisted queue; in-memory rejected because it breaks the upload-and-walk-away promise across restarts" is what stops the question reopening. A row is enough here; this does not need a separate decision doc.
- **Rank the tenets (F9).** This collision is between your first and third tenets. Ordering them in the HLD settles this case and the next one like it.

I haven't edited anything; coaching is advisory. Once you know which way you want to go, running `/linked-intent-dev` with a one-line description of the change (for example, "persist the job queue across restarts") will walk it from the HLD down through the LLD and specs in order.
