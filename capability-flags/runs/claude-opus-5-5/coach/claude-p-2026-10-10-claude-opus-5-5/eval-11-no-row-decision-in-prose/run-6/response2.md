**F1 — High. The queue's storage design pulls against your lead tenet.** `job-queue-design.md:14` holds the queue "entirely in process memory" and says that jobs accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart "are gone, and members resubmit them". Line 10 of the same doc says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip". Together these mean a member who uploads at 9pm can find at 8am that an overnight reboot dropped the job and nothing printed. This is where *tenet cascade* applies (leaf designs should lean the way the HLD's tenets say). The HLD's first tenet is "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away", and its first goal is "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present". The restart is not an edge case, because your own Context section describes it as routine, and it lands in the overnight window the goal is built around. It matters beyond the lost print: a future session reading only the HLD will assume accepted jobs are durable, while one reading only the LLD will faithfully build the loss in, and nothing on the page says which is intended. The specs have the same gap: QUEUE-004 covers rebuilding the state of running printers on startup, but no spec covers what happens to waiting jobs.

There are three ways to resolve it, and the choice is yours:

- **Change the design.** Persist accepted jobs so they survive a restart. The G-code is already on disk under a content hash and the registry already keeps a SQLite file on the same machine, so the missing piece is the queue entries and their order. The cost is a little more state to keep coherent, against "the service stays small".
- **Record the exception.** Keep the in-memory queue and add a Decisions row saying that it trades against the "job is a promise" tenet, and why. Members then need to learn that a job was dropped, which the design does not currently provide for.
- **Revise the tenet.** If the real promise is weaker (say, "a dispatched job is a promise"), state that in the HLD, and soften the overnight goal to match.

**F2 — High. The choice has no Decisions row.** The Decisions & Alternatives table in `job-queue-design.md:29-33` covers scheduling order, job identifiers and the upload limit, and the first two are good examples: each names the rejected alternative and the accepted cost. The in-memory queue appears only in Architecture prose. This is *decision capture* (a choice a cold reader would still question needs what was given up and what cost was accepted on the page). A reader will question this one, because `printer-registry-design.md:25` already chose SQLite as a store that "fits the mini-PC". Without a row, the next agent to touch the queue either "fixes" it by adding persistence you may not want, or preserves a loss you never intended. A row is the right size of capture here; nothing else in the tree leans on this reasoning, so it does not need a decision doc.

**How the two connect.** If you record the exception for F1, that row closes F2 as well. If you change the design, the new row is "Queue store", with in-memory as the rejected alternative. If you revise the tenet, the row is still worth adding, because the SQLite question remains.

**Carrying it through.** Whichever you pick, the specs need to follow: either a spec that accepted jobs survive a restart, or one stating what a member sees when a job is dropped.
