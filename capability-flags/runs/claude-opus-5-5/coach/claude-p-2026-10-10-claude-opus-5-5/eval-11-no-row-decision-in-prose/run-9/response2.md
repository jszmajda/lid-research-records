**F1 — High. The queue design and your first tenet point in opposite directions.** `job-queue-design.md` (Architecture) holds the queue "entirely in process memory" and says that jobs "accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same doc's Context says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." So a member uploads at 9pm, the box reboots for an update at 2am, and their job no longer exists — and they find out when they arrive to collect a part. That runs against the HLD tenet "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" and the goal "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present". This is *tenet cascade*: leaf designs should lean the way the HLD's tenets say to lean, or record where they deliberately don't. It matters because a future session implementing the queue will read the leaf design, not the tenet, and build the lossy behaviour faithfully. The spec layer won't catch it either: QUEUE-004 only says the service rebuilds farm state by polling printers, and is silent on what happens to queued jobs.

There are three ways to close it, and the choice is yours:

- **Change the design.** Persist accepted jobs so they survive a restart. The G-code is already on disk under a content hash and the registry already keeps a SQLite file on the same machine, so the design has somewhere obvious to put them. The cost is a little more state to manage, weighed against "the service stays small".
- **Record the exception.** Keep the in-memory queue and add a Decisions row in `job-queue-design.md` that names the tenet being traded against and why. If you go this way, the design probably also owes members a way to learn their job was dropped; today nothing in it says they are told.
- **Revise the tenet.** If the real promise is weaker (say, "a job that has started printing will finish"), reword the tenet and the overnight goal in `docs/high-level-design.md` to say so.

**F2 — High. The choice itself isn't written down as a decision.** The Decisions & Alternatives table in `job-queue-design.md` has rows for scheduling order, job identifiers and the upload limit, but none for where the queue lives. That choice appears only as a clause in the Architecture prose. This is *decision capture*: a choice a cold reader would question or want to reverse should record what was given up and what cost was accepted. A reader would question this one, because the neighbouring node persists to SQLite and the uploads are already on disk, so in-memory looks like an oversight rather than a decision. Without a row, the next session either "fixes" it without knowing what was being protected, or preserves it without knowing whether it was meant. The remedy is a single fuller row, not a separate decision doc: chosen store, the rejected alternative (persisting alongside the registry), and the accepted cost. If you pick "record the exception" for F1, that same row closes F2. If you change the design, the row records the new choice instead.

**Two smaller things settle alongside these:**

- **Tenet order (F6).** "A job is a promise" and "the service stays small" are exactly the two tenets in tension here, and the HLD doesn't say which wins. Ordering the tenets would have decided this question before the design was written, and will decide the next one like it.
- **A spec for restart behaviour (part of F5).** Whichever way you go, the outcome for queued jobs on restart deserves its own `QUEUE` spec — either "jobs survive" or "jobs are dropped and the member is told" — so tests can hold it in place.

To apply any of this, run `/linked-intent-dev` with the direction you've chosen. It will walk the change from the HLD or design doc down through the specs, which is where the tenet wording, the Decisions row and the new spec get argued and written.
