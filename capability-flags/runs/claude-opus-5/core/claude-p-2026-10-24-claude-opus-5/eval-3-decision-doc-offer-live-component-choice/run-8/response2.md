Written to `docs/intent/record-store/decisions/concurrent-visit-edits.md`, owned by record-store and carrying no spec IDs.

**How it's built.** Context frames the problem from the domain — 48-hour offline windows, independent corrections to one visit, arrival order carrying no authority — and names the asymmetry the decision turns on: a held-back visit is visible and recoverable, a silently composed record ships to the partner as an observation.

Seven weighted criteria, no gates. Neither option is eliminated by a constraint — both retain originals, both can require coordinator approval of any composed record, neither needs botanical judgment from the server — so recording constraints they both pass would have been noise. Three are major and each names the tenet it derives from: composition safety (*don't silently discard a submitted edit*, plus the no-inference non-goal), maintenance burden of reconciliation logic (*workflows two coordinators can operate*), provenance of an exported value (*keep the path inspectable*). Review volume, export readiness, and submission size are the moderate-to-minor ones.

Both options get their strongest honest statement, each with a demonstration using the same example — observer-name correction on one tablet, missing species entry on another — so a cold reader sees exactly what the merge option would have bought: that visit ships the same day with nobody involved. Field-aware merge is scored strong on review volume, export readiness, and attention; it loses on the majors.

**Why we'd still consider merge** has its own part of Selection, written as conditions rather than regret: the choice assumes concurrent editing of one visit is occasional, and if unresolved visits become a routine Friday backlog, or resolutions turn out to be overwhelmingly "both edits were fine and unrelated," then automatic combination over a narrow enumerated field set earns its maintenance. The doc notes the migration stays open — originals retained, every submission naming its base revision — and names the two signals to watch: unresolved count at export time, and how often a resolution preserves both revisions' changes.

**What the downstream nodes must accommodate** is spelled out as obligation, not prediction. Tablet-sync: base revision ID per local draft, a submitted visit reported as *in competition* rather than accepted-and-done, and a path back to a visit whose revision lost — a stale base is a visit to look at again, not an error to retry. Review-export: a comparison view over competing revisions, a resolution action recording coordinator identity and contributing submission IDs, unresolved visits held out of the CSV, and therefore a row count that's a function of review progress.

**One thing I'd like your ruling on.** Probing for criteria that cut *against* your choice turned up one I kept, because I think it's real: **sustained review attention**. A queue that grows past what coordinators actually read converts into reflexive approval — which defeats composition safety by a different route than automatic merging does, and leaves no trace that it happened. Whole-visit revisions score only *partial* there (most items, each a whole-visit comparison); merge scores strong. I recorded it as a moderate criterion and put the mitigation on review-export as an obligation. Two questions: is moderate the right weight, or does this belong alongside the majors — and is "resolving is reading, not clicking through" something you want to become an actual review-export requirement later, rather than a line in this doc?
