**F1 — High. The memory-only queue works against the HLD's central promise, under a condition the design doc itself describes.** `job-queue-design.md:14` holds the queue "entirely in process memory," and states that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Four lines earlier, `job-queue-design.md:10` names the operating condition that makes this bite: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together on the project's own timeline — evening uploads, overnight dispatch — and an unattended reboot at 2am silently drops every job still waiting in line, with no member present to notice or resubmit. That lands against the HLD tenet *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away"* and against the goal *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* This is the *tenet cascade* lens — design nodes should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean, and where they can't, the exception goes on the record. Note that the restart-recovery spec you do have, `QUEUE-004`, rebuilds state by polling printers for their *current* job: it recovers what already dispatched, which is exactly the set that didn't need rescuing, and recovers nothing of the pending queue. Why this matters here: the tenet exists to pre-narrow the lean for decisions no spec anticipated, and right now a future session reading `job-queue-design.md` alone will read "members resubmit them" as settled intent and build more behavior on top of it — retry UX, notification policy, queue depth display — all of it quietly assuming job loss is acceptable, which the HLD says it isn't. The longer that sits, the more code inherits the assumption. Three remedies, and the choice is genuinely yours: **change the design** (the cheapest version is persisting accepted-but-undispatched jobs to the registry's existing SQLite file or a sibling one — the G-code already lives on disk under a content hash, so you're only durably recording queue membership and order, not payloads); **record the exception** as a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g., resubmission is cheap because uploads are content-hashed, and members are told so); or **revise the tenet** if "upload and walk away" was always meant to mean "walk away once dispatched." Recording the exception would also close F2 below.

**F2 — High. The durability choice has no Decisions & Alternatives row at all.** The job-queue table at `job-queue-design.md:29-33` captures three choices carefully — the shortest-job-first rejection with its gaming rationale and accepted cost, base32 slugs over UUIDs with the recycling cost named, the 64 MB limit with its source. The in-memory queue, which is the most consequential and most reversible-looking choice in the node, isn't there. Under *decision capture* — a choice a cold reader would still question once landed needs its rejected alternative and accepted cost on the page — this row is the gap, and the sibling node makes the contrast sharp: `printer-registry-design.md:14` persists far less critical state (printer id, class, address) to "a single SQLite file," with a row explaining why. A cold reader holding both docs will ask the obvious question — SQLite was already chosen, already on the mini-PC, already justified for this exact hardware, so why not here? — and find nothing answering it. Why this matters: without the row, the next session has two bad options, both of which cost you. It re-derives the reasoning from scratch and may get it wrong, or it reads the absence as oversight and "fixes" it by adding persistence, overturning a choice that may have been deliberate (process simplicity on modest hardware, per your third tenet) without knowing it was a choice. A row is cheap insurance against both. The recommended capture is the smallest thing that settles it: one row in the `## Decisions & Alternatives` table of `job-queue-design.md` — decision "Queue durability," chosen "in-process memory, rebuilt from printers on restart," rationale naming SQLite-backed queue as the rejected alternative, why it was rejected, and the accepted cost (pending jobs lost on restart; members resubmit). I'd stop at the row rather than a decision doc — nothing else in the tree currently restates or sizes itself around this reasoning, which is the condition that would make a full doc worth the weight.

**The two findings are one thread, and the order matters.** F2 is a documentation gap; F1 is a design question. If you decide the design should change, F2's row changes with it — you'd be recording the move *to* durability and what it cost (a write path on every accept, a second file to back up), rather than the move away. So I'd settle F1 first: decide whether "upload and walk away" is meant to survive an overnight reboot. If yes, persistence in `job-queue` is a small change and `QUEUE-004` grows a sibling spec for rebuilding the pending queue. If no, the tenet should say so, and the row should say why.
