Both findings are the same decision looked at from two directions, so I'll take them together.

**F1 — High. The queue's durability model works against the tenet and goal that the HLD puts first.** `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14` holds the per-class queue "entirely in process memory," and states that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same section's Context (line 10) names the operating conditions: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those two sentences side by side and the failure is in the docs already — a member uploads at 9pm, the mini-PC takes an update at 3am, and the job is gone with nobody present to notice, let alone resubmit. That is precisely the case the HLD's first tenet speaks to — "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" — and the first goal, "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." This is a *tenet cascade* concern (tenets are forward-looking tie-breakers that the design nodes beneath them are supposed to lean with, and the arrow is supposed to stay coherent between adjacent levels). Worth saying plainly: the two docs never share a word, which is why this kind of conflict survives review — "in process memory" and "upload and walk away" don't collide on the page, only in the overnight window. What compounds if it sits is that the tenet stops being load-bearing: a future session reading the queue node sees in-memory as settled intent, builds the next feature on top of it, and the promise in the HLD quietly becomes aspirational text that nothing downstream honors. Note that the *other* cost of FIFO — one long job holding a class for hours — is handled well in your Decisions table, with the alternative and the accepted cost both named; the durability trade got none of that treatment.

**F2 — High. The in-memory choice has no Decisions & Alternatives row.** The job-queue table at `job-queue-design.md:29-33` carries rows for scheduling order, job identifiers, and upload size limit, but nothing for where accepted jobs live — while the sibling node records exactly this kind of call ("Registry store | Single SQLite file | Fits the mini-PC; nothing else needs concurrent writers" at `printer-registry-design.md:25`). So the project persists printer rows to SQLite and drops member jobs on restart, and only one of those two decisions is on the record. This is *decision capture* (a choice that a cold reader would still question once landed needs enough of a row to reconstruct what was given up and what cost was accepted). The test here passes easily: any reader who holds the HLD's promise tenet and then reads "members resubmit them" will want to reverse the choice, and with no row they can't tell whether durability was weighed and rejected for a reason — maybe write-ahead to the same SQLite file was considered too heavy for the mini-PC, maybe the 90-day slug recycling made recovery messy — or whether it was simply never considered. Those lead to opposite actions: the first says leave it alone, the second says fix it. A future session will guess, and half the time it will guess wrong. One row would settle it permanently; this doesn't warrant a decision doc.

**What I'd recommend**

For F1 the remedy is genuinely your call, and the three options are different projects:

- **Change the design** — give accepted-but-undispatched jobs a durable home. Your registry already has a SQLite file on the box, and the G-code itself is already on disk under a content hash, so what's missing is just the queue entry; this is the option that makes the tenet true rather than aspirational.
- **Record the exception** — keep in-memory, and add a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g. that tenet 3, "the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small," wins here, and that restarts are rare enough to accept resubmission). This is honest and cheap, and it also closes F2 in the same stroke.
- **Revise the tenet** — if walk-away durability isn't actually the promise you want to make, soften it in the HLD so the design and the tenet agree at the top instead of at the leaf.

That F1/F2 interaction is worth being deliberate about: the exception-row option resolves both findings at once, but if you pick the design change instead, F2 still wants a row — the one that records *why* jobs became durable and what it cost on modest hardware.

Two things that fall out of this either way. **F8** is the same decision in disguise: tenet 1 (the promise) and tenet 3 (stay small) collide head-on here, and your tenets are unordered, so the HLD gives no tie-breaker for exactly the choice the queue node had to make. Ordering them — or merging the pair into one tenet that states the lean — would mean the next decision in this family doesn't need a coach to notice it. And **QUEUE-004** ("rebuild farm state by polling each registered printer") exists only because of the in-memory choice; if you go the durability route, that spec and the Architecture paragraph above it move together, within one segment, so the cascade is contained.
