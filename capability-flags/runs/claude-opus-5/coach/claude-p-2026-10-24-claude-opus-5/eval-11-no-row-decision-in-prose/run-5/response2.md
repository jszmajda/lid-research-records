**F1 — High. The job-queue holds accepted jobs only in process memory, and the restart it describes is the one the HLD promises to survive.** `job-queue-design.md:14` is explicit: the queue is "a per-printer-class list of accepted jobs held entirely in process memory," and "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The condition that triggers that loss is named two paragraphs earlier, in the node's own Context: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip," and jobs "dispatch overnight as printers free up." Those two statements intersect at exactly the worst moment — an unattended 2 a.m. update lands while four jobs sit undispatched behind a long print, and the member who uploaded at 9 p.m. arrives in the morning to no part and no notification, needing to resubmit. The HLD's first tenet is *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away,"* and its first goal is *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* Under *tenet cascade* — design nodes should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean, and an exception should be recorded where it's taken — this is a conflict rather than a cost: the member isn't getting a slower promise, they're getting the promise withdrawn silently, and "resubmit" requires the presence the goal says they shouldn't need. Note this isn't a theoretical durability quibble; the registry node already runs SQLite on the same box (`printer-registry-design.md:14`), so the capability the queue forgoes is sitting in the next node over. What makes it worth surfacing now rather than later is that the in-memory assumption is load-bearing for the startup design too — QUEUE-004's "rebuild farm state by polling each registered printer" exists *because* nothing was persisted, so the longer it sits, the more of the node is shaped around it.

**F2 — High. That choice isn't in the Decisions & Alternatives table at all.** The table at `job-queue-design.md:27–33` is good work — it records scheduling order, job identifiers, and the upload size limit, each naming the rejected alternative and the accepted cost ("UUIDs rejected: unreadable on printer LCDs and in chat; accepted cost: slugs recycle after 90 days"). The durability of accepted jobs, which is the choice with the largest user-visible consequence in the node, has no row. Under *docs carry current intent* — a doc should read as if authored fresh today, and a decision that a cold reader would still question needs its reasoning on the page — the test is forward from the landed design: a reader holding this node, the HLD's tenets, and the knowledge that SQLite is already in the stack will stop at line 14 and want to reverse it. The prose states the consequence but never the reasoning, so the next session can't tell whether in-memory was a deliberate trade against the third tenet ("the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small") or just what got built first. That ambiguity is expensive in a specific way: an agent that reads it as accidental will "fix" it unasked, and an agent that reads it as deliberate will build more on top of it — and both are reasonable readings of what's currently written.

**What I'd recommend**

The two findings have one remedy each, and one of the options closes both. For the tenet conflict, the choice is yours and I'd rather lay out the three than pick for you:

- **Change the design** — persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs. The queue already writes G-code to disk under a content hash, so a jobs table in a SQLite file (or alongside the registry's) makes restart recovery a read rather than a poll, and QUEUE-004's printer polling narrows to reconciling what's *running*, which is the part only the printer knows. This is the option that keeps the tenet intact, and it's the one I'd lean toward given that the loss window is unattended overnight hours.
- **Record the exception** — keep in-memory, and add a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why: something in the shape of *"Accepted-job durability | In-memory only | Persistence rejected to keep the service small on the mini-PC (tenet 3); accepted cost: jobs accepted but undispatched are lost on restart, and members must resubmit — trades against the upload-and-walk-away promise during unattended updates."* That's an honest answer, and it also closes F2, since the row is exactly the capture that finding asks for.
- **Revise the tenet** — if "upload and walk away" was always meant as best-effort, say so in the HLD, and the conflict dissolves. This is the least likely of the three given how the Problem section describes member pain, but it's a legitimate call.

Worth flagging: if you take the first option, F2 still needs a row — the decision to *persist* is just as worth capturing as the decision not to, and it's the row that stops a future session from re-litigating. A row is the right size here either way; this doesn't warrant a decision doc, since there's no option space a future reader would need to re-run.

Two related items travel with this one if you want to handle the thread together: **F10** (the tenets are unordered, yet "a submitted job is a promise" and "the service stays small" are the two sides of exactly this trade — ordering them pre-settles the next conflict of this shape), and the fact that whichever way you go, there's currently **no spec** covering restart behavior for accepted jobs. QUEUE-004 specs the printer polling but nothing specs what happens to the queue itself, so the behavior at the center of this finding is unverifiable either way.

When you've decided, `/linked-intent-dev` with a description of the change is the right entry point — it'll cascade from the Decisions row down through the spec and into tests and code, rather than leaving the design and spec layers to drift apart. I'd avoid hand-editing just line 14; the row, the spec, and the startup design need to move together.
