**F1 — High. The queue's volatility works against your first tenet under a condition the design itself names.** `job-queue-design.md:14` holds the queue "entirely in process memory," and says that jobs accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart "are gone, and members resubmit them." Four lines earlier, the same Context section tells us the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" — so restarts are not an exceptional event in this deployment, they're expected. Put those together with the HLD's first tenet, *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away,"* and its first goal, *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present,"* and the mechanism defeats both precisely in the window they exist to cover: a member uploads at 9pm, an update restarts the service at 2am, and the only actor who can recover the job is the one the design promised wouldn't need to be there. This is *tenet cascade* — the check that each design node leans the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean. The two never share wording (a tenet about promises, an architecture sentence about process memory), which is exactly why this kind of conflict survives review; what makes it a finding rather than a cost is that the described operating condition turns it from "spends something against the goal" into "defeats it." Left as-is, the gap compounds in a specific way: future sessions reading `job-queue-design.md` will build retry, notification, and UI behavior on top of an acceptance guarantee that the HLD promises and the node doesn't keep, and each layer makes the eventual reconciliation more expensive. I'd note too that your third tenet — "the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small" — leans the other way, which is real: this is a genuine tenet collision, not a simple oversight, and it's the same collision behind F8's suggestion to order the tenets.

**F2 — High. The decision to keep the queue in memory has no row in Decisions & Alternatives.** The table at `job-queue-design.md:27-33` captures three choices well — FIFO-within-two-classes names shortest-job-first as the rejected alternative and "one long job can hold a class for hours" as the accepted cost; base32 slugs name UUIDs and slug recycling. Those rows are the model. The volatility of the queue is a larger choice than any of them and appears only as narration in the Architecture paragraph, with no alternative named and no cost accepted — made sharper by the asymmetry sitting one sentence later, where the *G-code payloads* are persisted to disk under a content hash while the queue that references them is not, and by the sibling node persisting its registry to SQLite on the same box. That asymmetry is what makes this *decision capture* under *docs carry current intent*: the test isn't whether the call was hard, it's whether a cold reader holding the landed design would still question it or be tempted to reverse it — and a reader who has just been told the disk is already in use, SQLite is already a dependency, and restarts are expected will reach for the fix immediately, with nothing on the page telling them why you didn't. The capture finding stands independently of F1: even if you keep the design exactly as it is, the next session needs to know it was chosen rather than defaulted into.

**What I'd recommend**

For F1, the remedy is yours to pick, and all three are legitimate:

- **Change the design** — persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs (a small table in the existing SQLite file, or a journal file beside the hashed uploads) so the queue survives a restart and the tenet holds literally.
- **Record the exception** — keep the in-memory queue and add a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why: that the window of exposure is small, that resubmission is cheap because the hashed upload is still on disk, that the mini-PC budget argued against another writer. If you go this way, consider whether the promise is better kept at the edges instead — e.g. notifying the member that a job was lost, which turns a silent failure into a recoverable one.
- **Revise the tenet** — if "upload and walk away" is really an aspiration rather than a tie-breaker you want future sessions to lean on, say so at the HLD level rather than leaving the leaf to quietly contradict it.

Worth knowing: the second option also closes F2, since the row that records the exception is the same row that captures the decision. If you choose either of the others, F2 still wants its own row — the smallest thing that settles it is one line in the existing table naming the rejected alternative (persisting the queue alongside the registry) and the accepted cost (jobs accepted within a restart window are lost and must be resubmitted). That's the right size for this; a decision doc would be overkill. The one thing that might argue for more is that QUEUE-004's startup rebuild, the content-hash storage, and the resubmission expectation all size themselves around this choice — if you want the full option space on record because more behavior will lean on it, a decision doc is available, though I'd still start with the row.

Either way, the editing happens in your hands or through `/linked-intent-dev` if you'd rather walk the change forward as a cascade — the coach doesn't touch files, and this is exactly the kind of decision where your review is the point.
