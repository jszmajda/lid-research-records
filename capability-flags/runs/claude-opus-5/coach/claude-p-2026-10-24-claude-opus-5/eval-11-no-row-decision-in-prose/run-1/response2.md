## The durability knot

**F1 — High. The queue's memory-only design works against the HLD's central promise under a condition the LLD itself names.** `job-queue-design.md:14` holds the per-class queue "entirely in process memory," and says that on restart "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Two lines earlier, the Context section describes the mini-PC as taking "unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" — so the triggering condition isn't hypothetical, it's documented operating reality. The HLD's first tenet is *"a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away,"* and the first goal is *"evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* A 2 a.m. update reboot drops every queued-but-undispatched job, and the member who walked away finds out in the morning — and can't resubmit, because nobody is present. That's the *tenet cascade* lens (design nodes should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean, or record the exception). The two don't share a word of vocabulary — "promise" and "process memory" never collide on the page — which is exactly why this kind of conflict survives a normal read. What makes it worth fixing now rather than later: the queue's durability model is the thing the dispatch design, the restart rebuild (`QUEUE-004`), and the resubmit-is-cheap content-hash storage are all shaped around, so the cost of revisiting it rises with every spec that assumes it.

**F2 — High. The same choice has no row in Decisions & Alternatives.** The job-queue table at `job-queue-design.md:29-33` captures three decisions well — the FIFO row even names the rejected alternative (shortest-job-first), why it was rejected (members game it), and the accepted cost (one long job holds a class). The durability choice, which is the more consequential of the four, appears only as narrative architecture prose. Under *docs carry current intent* (a doc reads as if authored fresh today, by someone who knows the intent and nothing of the conversations behind it), the test is forward from the landed design: would a cold reader still question this or want to reverse it? Here they plainly would — they'd read the tenet, read line 14, and have no way to tell whether the volatility was weighed and accepted or simply never examined. That ambiguity is expensive in a specific way: the next session that meets it either re-derives the whole argument, or "fixes" it by adding persistence and silently contradicts a constraint (the third tenet — the mini-PC is modest, the service stays small) that may well have been the real reason.

**F5 and F8 are the same knot from two other sides.** The registry persists to SQLite (`printer-registry-design.md:14`) while the queue doesn't, and nothing at the HLD explains the asymmetry — under *intent attaches at the lowest node that dominates it*, a durability stance that governs both nodes belongs at their shared parent, not absent from both. And tenet 1 ("a submitted job is a promise") and tenet 3 ("the mini-PC is modest; the service stays small") are the two tenets that collide here, listed unordered — so a future session meeting this trade-off cold gets no lean from the HLD, which is precisely the job tenets exist to do.

## What I'd recommend

For the tenet conflict (F1), three remedies are legitimate and the choice is yours:

- **Change the design** — give accepted jobs a durable home. A write-ahead of queue entries into the registry's existing SQLite file is the small-footprint option: the G-code is already on disk under a content hash, so what's lost in a restart is just the ordering and ownership metadata, which is cheap to persist and respects tenet 3.
- **Record the exception** — keep the memory-only queue and add a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g. the resubmit cost is low because the file is already stored; blips are rare; durability isn't worth the write path on this hardware). This also closes F2.
- **Revise the tenet** — if the promise was always meant as best-effort, say so in the HLD so downstream nodes stop inheriting a stronger commitment than you intend.

If you keep the current design, that one Decisions row handles both F1 and F2 — this is a table-row decision, not a decision doc; the three existing rows are exactly the right resolution to match. If you change the design, it's a table row plus a spec: there's currently no EARS coverage for restart durability at all, and `QUEUE-004`'s rebuild behavior would need to say what happens to persisted-but-undispatched jobs.

Either way, I'd also add a line to the HLD stating the farm-wide durability stance — what survives a restart and what doesn't — and order the tenets so the promise/small-service trade has a declared lean. That's the piece that keeps this from being re-litigated by a future session.
