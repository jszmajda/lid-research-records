Here are the two findings in detail — they're the same choice seen from two angles, so they're worth reading together.

**F1 — High. The queue's durability model works against the promise tenet under a condition the design itself names.** `job-queue-design.md:14` holds accepted jobs "entirely in process memory," and states the consequence plainly: "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Four lines earlier, the same Context section establishes the operating condition that makes this bite: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together on the project's own timeline — upload in the evening, dispatch overnight as printers free up — and a job that waits behind a 6-hour print sits in volatile memory through exactly the window when nobody is watching. An unattended update at 2am and the member arrives next morning to no part, no print, and no notice. That runs against the HLD's first tenet, "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," and against the goal "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." This is *tenet cascade* — the HLD's tenets are the lean a future session takes when no spec covers the call, so a leaf mechanism that leans the other way quietly overrides them. Note that the two documents never share a word here: "promise" and "held entirely in process memory" don't look like they're about the same thing, which is precisely why this kind of conflict survives review. Why it matters for printfarm specifically: the tenet is doing real work in this project — it's the line a future session would consult when deciding whether to add retry, acknowledgement, or notification behavior. While the queue contradicts it, each of those future decisions gets argued from scratch, and the member-facing promise erodes one plausible-looking choice at a time. There's also a narrower gap inside the same behavior: `QUEUE-004` specs rebuilding farm state from printers on startup, but nothing specs what members are told about the jobs that didn't survive — the loss is currently silent by omission.

**F2 — High. The durability choice has no row in the Decisions & Alternatives table, while its sibling node explains the same kind of choice.** `job-queue-design.md:27-33` captures three decisions well — FIFO-within-two-classes names shortest-job-first as the rejected alternative *and* the accepted cost ("one long job can hold a class for hours"), and the slug row does the same. In-memory-with-no-persistence isn't among them, even though it's the choice with the largest member-visible consequence in the node. Meanwhile `printer-registry-design.md:25` does record its persistence choice ("Single SQLite file — Fits the mini-PC; nothing else needs concurrent writers"). So a cold reader gets a project where the printer table is durable and deliberately so, the job queue is volatile and unexplained, and the same SQLite file is sitting right there. They will question it, and reasonably assume it's an oversight to fix rather than a judgment to respect. Under *docs carry current intent* — rationale and accepted costs that a fresh author would still write today are live intent, not residue — this is an under-captured decision: the test isn't whether the call was hard, it's whether a reader holding the landed design would still want to reverse it. What compounds if it sits: the next session either re-derives the tradeoff (burning tokens on a question already settled) or "fixes" it by bolting on persistence that ignores whatever constraint actually drove the original call. A row costs one line and ends both failure modes permanently.

**What I'd recommend**

For F2 the remedy is small and I'd do it regardless of how you resolve F1: add a Decisions row for the queue's durability model, naming what was rejected and the cost accepted — the shape your FIFO row already uses. Something in the spirit of *"Queue durability | In-memory, no persistence | Persisting accepted jobs rejected: <the actual reason — surface area on modest hardware? restarts assumed rare?>; accepted cost: undispatched jobs are lost on restart and members resubmit."* The reason has to be yours, not mine — that's the piece that only exists in your head right now, and it's the whole value of the row. I wouldn't reach for a decision doc here; nothing else in the design tree restates or sizes itself around this choice, so a row is the right resolution.

For F1 the resolution is yours, and there are three legitimate ways out — I'm deliberately not picking:

- **Change the design.** Persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs. The registry's SQLite file is already there, G-code is already on disk under a content hash, so the durable state is small — slug, class, priority, hash, accepted-at. This would touch `job-queue-design.md`'s Architecture section and likely add a spec for restoring the queue on startup alongside `QUEUE-004`.
- **Record the exception.** Keep the in-memory queue and add the Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why — e.g., that restarts are rare enough, or resubmission cheap enough, to accept the promise being best-effort. This also closes F2 outright, since it's the same row.
- **Revise the tenet.** If upload-and-walk-away was always aspirational for the mini-PC, the honest move may be softening the tenet in the HLD so it stops pointing future sessions at a guarantee the service doesn't make.

There's a middle path worth considering under the first option if full persistence feels like too much machinery for the "stays small" tenet: spec a member-facing notice for jobs lost to a restart. That keeps the queue volatile but makes the failure loud instead of silent, which is the part of the promise that actually stings.

Worth noting that F8 from the report is the structural reason this slipped: your three tenets are an unordered list, and "a submitted job is a promise" versus "the service stays small" pull opposite ways here. Whichever way you resolve F1, ordering those two gives the next session the tie-breaker this decision didn't have.
