Here's the durability thread in detail — three findings that are really one knot.

**F1 — High. The in-memory queue works against the project's first tenet under a condition the design doc itself describes.** `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14` states the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Four lines earlier, the Context section names the operating condition that makes this bite: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together and the overnight scenario the HLD was written for — upload at 9pm, restart at 2am, member arrives at 9am — ends with no print and no notice. That runs against the tenet *"A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away"* and against the goal *"Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present."* This is the *tenet cascade* lens — leaf designs should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean, and where they can't, the exception goes on the record. The reason it matters more than a typical durability gap is that "resubmit" silently relocates the cost onto exactly the person the tenet promised wouldn't have to be present; the failure is invisible until morning, so nobody learns the queue is lossy until it has already cost someone a night. Worth noting that no Decisions row anywhere names a tenet being traded against here, which is what makes it read as drift rather than a deliberate call.

**F2 — High. That choice has no Decisions & Alternatives row at all.** `job-queue-design.md:27-33` captures three decisions — scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size limit — and captures them well: each names the rejected alternative *and* the accepted cost ("members game it by splitting prints"; "slugs recycle after 90 days"). The durability model, which is the larger choice of the four, appears only as narrative prose in the Architecture section. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — a doc should stand on its own for a reader who wasn't in the conversation that produced it — the test is whether a cold reader would still question the choice or be tempted to reverse it. Here they plainly would, and the sibling node makes it sharper: `printer-registry-design.md:14` already persists to "a single SQLite file," and its own row says "nothing else needs concurrent writers." A future session reading both nodes sees durable storage sitting right there in the stack, unused by the queue, with no stated reason. That session will either re-derive the argument from scratch or quietly "fix" it — and if the real reason was the third tenet ("the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small"), that reasoning is currently nowhere on the page. A fuller Decisions row is the right size of capture here; a decision doc would be over-production, since no other node leans on this reasoning or sizes itself around it.

**F10 — Low, but it's the root of the other two.** The HLD's three tenets are an unordered list, and tenets 1 and 3 pull in opposite directions precisely at this decision: "a submitted job is a promise" argues for durable accepted-job state, "the service stays small" argues against another moving part. *Tenets capture forward-looking preference* — a tenet is a tie-breaker for the decision no spec anticipated — and an unordered pair that can conflict doesn't break the tie; it just presents the next session with the same fork you're looking at now. Ordering them (or adding a clause to one that says which yields) would have made the durability call fall out rather than needing to be argued.

## What I'd recommend

For **F1**, the remedy is yours to pick, and all three are legitimate:

- **Change the design** — persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs. The cheapest version is a table in the SQLite file the registry already maintains, or a sibling file; the G-code is already on disk under a content hash, so only the queue entries need to survive. This honors tenet 1 at a small cost to tenet 3.
- **Record the exception** — keep the in-memory queue and add a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g. restarts are rare enough, and a lost-job notice at upload time is cheaper than a persistence layer). This also closes F2.
- **Revise the tenet** — if "upload and walk away" was always meant as best-effort, say so in the HLD so downstream nodes stop inheriting a stronger promise than you intend.

If you take the first option, note that it's a change in intent, not a doc cleanup — run it through `/linked-intent-dev` so it cascades properly: the Architecture section of the job-queue node, a new `QUEUE-` spec for restart recovery of undispatched jobs (`QUEUE-004` currently only covers rebuilding from *printers*, which is a different thing), and the tests below it.

For **F2** specifically, if you take the second option, the row wants the same shape as your existing three — chosen, rejected alternative, accepted cost — something along the lines of: *Accepted-job durability | In-process memory | SQLite persistence rejected: a second writer and a recovery path on modest hardware; accepted cost: a restart before dispatch loses queued jobs and members resubmit.* Then F1 and F2 both close, and a cold reader stops having to wonder.

For **F10**, ordering the tenet list is a one-line edit to `docs/high-level-design.md:18-22` — and it's worth doing whichever way F1 lands, because the same fork will come up again the next time "small" collides with "reliable."
