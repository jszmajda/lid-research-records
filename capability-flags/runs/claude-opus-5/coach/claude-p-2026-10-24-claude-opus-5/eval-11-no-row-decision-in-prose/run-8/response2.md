**F1 — High. The queue's in-memory design works against the HLD's own promise, under conditions the queue's own Context describes.** `job-queue-design.md:14` holds the per-class queue "entirely in process memory," and states that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Four lines earlier, the same doc's Context names the operating reality: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together and the failure is concrete — a member uploads at 21:00, an unattended update restarts the service at 03:00, and the job is simply gone; `QUEUE-004` rebuilds farm state by polling printers for what they're *running*, which by construction cannot recover a job that was accepted but never dispatched. Nobody is in the building to resubmit. That lands directly against the tenet "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" and the goal "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." This is *tenet cascade* — design nodes should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean, and when a mechanism defeats one under a condition the docs themselves describe, either the design or the record needs to move. Why it matters here: the tenet exists precisely to pre-settle the calls no spec anticipated, so a future session planning changes in this node will read "the queue lives in memory, members resubmit" as settled intent and build more on top of it — recovery UX, retry semantics, maybe a dispatch cache — all leaning away from the promise the HLD makes. The longer it sits, the more downstream design assumes the weaker guarantee, and the harder the promise is to restore. It's also a near-miss rather than a deep problem: the registry already persists to "a single SQLite file" on the same box, so durability is a solved problem one node over.

**F2 — High. That same choice has no Decisions & Alternatives row.** The job-queue table records three decisions — scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size limit — and each names what was rejected and the cost accepted (members gaming shortest-job-first; UUIDs unreadable on printer LCDs; slug recycling at 90 days). The durability choice, which is more consequential than any of them, appears only as narrative prose in the Architecture section. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — and its keep-side, that rationale and considered alternatives are present intent rather than residue — this is an under-captured decision. The test is forward from the landed design: a cold reader holding this doc and the HLD above it would absolutely still question it, and would want to reverse it, since the obvious alternative (write accepted jobs to the registry's SQLite file, or a second one beside it) is sitting right there in the sibling node. Without the row they can't tell whether in-memory was a considered trade against the "service stays small" tenet or just where the first implementation landed, so every future session re-opens the question from scratch — or worse, assumes it was decided carefully and leaves it alone.

**F9 — Medium, and it's the reason F1 happened.** The HLD's three tenets are an unordered list, but the first (the upload-and-walk-away promise) and the third ("the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small") pull in opposite directions exactly at the durability question — persistence is the kind of thing that makes a service bigger. *Tenets capture forward-looking preference*: they're one-line tie-breakers for decisions with two defensible answers and no spec covering them, and the audit signal for an unordered list is precisely more than two tenets that could conflict. Ordering them would have pre-narrowed this call rather than leaving the session that wrote `job-queue-design.md` to pick. Note that durability here is genuinely cheap — a few writes to a SQLite file the project already runs — so "stays small" may not even be in real tension once it's examined; that's worth knowing before you order them.

## What I'd recommend

For **F1**, the choice is yours, and all three are legitimate:

- **Change the design** — persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs. The G-code is already on disk under a content hash, so what's missing is the queue entry: job slug, target class, priority, submitter, submit time. The registry's SQLite file is already there, and "nothing else needs concurrent writers" is already in its Decisions table. This is the option that honors the tenet as written, and it's small.
- **Record the exception** — keep in-memory, and add a Decisions row that names the tenet being traded against and why ("accepted cost: a restart before dispatch loses queued jobs and the member must resubmit; mitigated by X"). Honest, and it makes the gap legible to the next session. Worth noting that this also closes F2 — one row settles both findings.
- **Revise the tenet** — if walk-away is actually aspirational rather than a promise you intend to keep at this stage, say so in the HLD and the conflict dissolves.

My read is that the first option is the likely fit, because the ingredients are already in the project and the tenet language ("a promise") is unusually strong. But that's your call, not mine.

If you change the design, the cascade runs through the segment: `job-queue-design.md` Architecture and Decisions, then a new `QUEUE` spec for restart recovery of queued jobs — something along the lines of *"When the service starts, the system SHALL restore accepted-but-undispatched jobs to their class queues"* — and `QUEUE-004` probably wants rewording, since "rebuild farm state by polling each registered printer" is currently the whole of restart intent. Persisting queue entries also reaches across the node boundary into the registry's store, so that's a pause-and-confirm rather than a free cascade: whether the queue shares the registry's SQLite file or gets its own is a decision the registry node has a stake in.

For **F2** on its own, the remedy is the smallest capture that settles the question — a fuller Decisions row, in the same shape as the three already in that table. A decision doc would be overkill; nothing else in the project restates this choice's reasoning or sizes itself around it.

For **F9**, ordering the tenets is a direct HLD edit. Do it after you've settled F1, so the order reflects the call you actually made.

All of these are edits you make in `docs/` — the coach stays advisory and doesn't apply them. If you'd rather walk the change forward as a real LID cycle, with the cascade and the boundary pause handled in order, `/linked-intent-dev` with a description of the durability change is the way in.
