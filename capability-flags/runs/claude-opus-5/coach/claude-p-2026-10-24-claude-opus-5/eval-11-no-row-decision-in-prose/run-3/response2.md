**F1 — High. The queue's in-memory architecture works against the promise tenet under a condition the same document describes.** `job-queue-design.md:14` holds accepted jobs "entirely in process memory," and states that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Four lines earlier, the Context paragraph names the operating conditions that make restarts routine rather than hypothetical: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put those together with the submission pattern the HLD describes — members upload "mostly in the evening" and jobs "dispatch overnight as printers free up" — and the window where jobs sit undispatched in memory is exactly the window where unattended restarts happen. The HLD's first tenet says "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," and its first goal is "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." A member who uploads at 9pm and walks away can find, in the morning, that no print happened and no one told them — the promise silently didn't hold, and the only recovery path requires the member to be present to notice. This is *tenet cascade* — the leaf designs should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean — and the conflict is the kind that rarely shares vocabulary with the tenet it breaks: nothing in the architecture paragraph mentions promises, and nothing in the tenet mentions process memory, so it only surfaces when you hold both at once. What makes it worth acting on rather than noting is that the failure is silent and the affected member is by definition absent; a dispatch failure has `QUEUE-003` to catch it, but an accepted-then-forgotten job has nothing. Worth saying explicitly: nothing here tells me the in-memory choice is *wrong* — the third tenet ("the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small") is a real pull in the other direction, and a makerspace that tells members "resubmit if the box reboots" is a defensible call. What's missing is any sign the two tenets were weighed.

**F4 — Medium. That choice has no row in Decisions & Alternatives, and the sibling node's opposite choice makes the silence louder.** The table at `job-queue-design.md:29` is genuinely good — the FIFO row names shortest-job-first as the rejected alternative, why it was rejected (members game it by splitting prints), and the accepted cost (one long job can hold a class for hours); the slug row does the same. The in-memory queue gets no row at all, even though it is a larger commitment than either. And a cold reader hits the asymmetry immediately: `printer-registry-design.md:14` persists the registry to "a single SQLite file" on the same mini-PC, for data that is cheaper to lose — printer state rebuilds from the next heartbeat round, whereas a lost job needs a human to notice and act. So the reader sees durable storage already present in the process, declined for the data that most needs it, with no stated reason. Under *decision capture* — a decision that stays live once landed, where a cold reader would still question or want to reverse it, should carry its reasoning on the page — this is the clearest instance in the project. The test isn't whether the call was contested; it's whether the next session can reconstruct it. Right now they can't, so the likely outcomes are that someone "fixes" it by adding a jobs table without knowing what was traded, or re-litigates the whole thing from scratch. A row costs one line and settles it permanently.

## What I'd recommend

For F1, three remedies are available and the choice is yours — I'd rather not pick it for you, because it turns on how the makerspace actually feels about a lost overnight print:

- **Change the design.** Persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs. The cheapest version adds a jobs table to the SQLite file the registry already owns — though note that crosses a node boundary, so it's a decision for the shared parent (the HLD's Architecture section) about who owns persistence, not something to lodge in the queue leaf alone. G-code is already on disk under a content hash, so what needs persisting is just queue membership and order.
- **Record the exception.** Keep the design and add a Decisions row naming the tenet traded against and why — something like: *Queue durability | In-memory only | Persistence rejected: keeps the service small per the mini-PC tenet; accepted cost: undispatched jobs are lost on restart, trading against "a submitted job is a promise" — members resubmit.* If you take this one, consider whether a spec should make the loss visible rather than silent (notify on startup that N accepted jobs were dropped), since that's what keeps the promise tenet partially intact.
- **Revise the tenet.** If the real intent is weaker than "upload and walk away," say so in the HLD — e.g. a promise that holds for dispatched jobs, with resubmission expected after a restart. That's a legitimate answer; it just needs to be the written one.

For F4, the remedy is the smallest capture that settles the question: a fuller Decisions row, not a decision doc. Nothing else in the project restates this choice's reasoning, so there's no case for putting a debate on the record — one row carries it. And note the overlap: **the second F1 remedy closes F4 as well**, since recording the exception *is* the missing row. If you instead change the design, you'll still want a row explaining why persistence won over staying small — either way F4 resolves with one table entry.

A related thread worth pulling once you've decided: F7 (the tenets are an unordered list) is this same collision in its upstream form. Tenet 1 and tenet 3 point opposite ways here, and whichever way you resolve F1 is the ordering — making it explicit in the HLD means the next session that meets a durability-versus-footprint question doesn't have to re-derive your answer.
