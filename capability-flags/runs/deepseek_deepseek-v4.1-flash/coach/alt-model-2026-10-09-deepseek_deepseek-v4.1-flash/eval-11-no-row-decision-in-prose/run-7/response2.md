**F1 — High. The queue's in-memory design pulls against one of your own tenets, and nothing records the trade.** In `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14`, the queue "is a per-printer-class list of accepted jobs held entirely in process memory," and the same paragraph states plainly that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Your HLD tenet says the opposite of that: "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" (`docs/high-level-design.md:20`), and the goal is "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present" (`:26`). The job-queue LLD's own Context even names the trigger — the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" (`:10`). So under an operating condition your docs describe, a member uploads, walks away, the box reboots, and their job is simply gone — exactly the promise the tenet makes. Under *tenet cascade* — a design should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean, and where it can't, the exception should be on the record — this is the pattern to catch. The reason it matters is that a tenet is a decision made in advance for exactly the moment no spec covers; when a mechanism quietly contradicts one, every future session that reads the LLD alone inherits the conflict without seeing it, and the re-submit behavior becomes the silent default rather than a deliberate concession. You have three ways to resolve it, and the choice is yours. You could **change the design** — persist accepted jobs (you already run SQLite for the registry, so the mini-PC clearly has room) so a restart rebuilds the queue the way `QUEUE-004` already rebuilds farm state. You could **record the exception** — a Decisions & Alternatives row naming the promise tenet traded away, why volatile memory was chosen, and what resubmission is expected to cost members. Or you could **revise the tenet** — narrow "upload and walk away" to "upload and walk away, except across a restart." Recording the exception is also the cleanest way to close F3, since both findings point at the same decision.

Under *decision capture* — a doc should carry the decisions a cold reader, holding the design and the docs above it, would still want to reverse or understand — this is a decision that stays live after it lands, so it belongs in the record rather than in prose. The reason it matters is that the table is where a future session goes to find "why did we accept this cost?"; without a row, the next reader either re-litigates the choice or, worse, assumes it was an accident to be fixed and quietly reverses intent — which is the exact drift LID exists to prevent.
