Here's the detailed walkthrough on the in-memory queue — that's three of the findings (F1, F3, F4), because they're really one choice seen from three angles.

**F1 — High. The design's lossy queue defeats a stated tenet, with no exception on the record.** `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14` says the queue lives "entirely in process memory," and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The HLD at `docs/high-level-design.md:20` states the tenet *"A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away."* The LLD's own Context (`job-queue-design.md:10`) tells us the operating condition under which this bites — the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." So a member who uploads in the evening, exactly the flow the project exists to serve, can have that accepted job silently vanish before morning. Under *tenets capture forward-looking preference* (a tenet is a one-line tie-breaker for choices no spec anticipated), the problem isn't that the design loses jobs — it's that the loss is neither reconciled with the tenet nor recorded as a deliberate exception. Why it matters: the tenet is the thing future sessions reach for when no spec covers a case, and right now it points the opposite way from the design; a reader who trusts the tenet will assume durability the design doesn't provide.

**F3 — Medium. The lossy-queue choice isn't captured as a decision.** Every other significant call in this LLD lands in the Decisions & Alternatives table (`job-queue-design.md:29-33`) with its rejected alternative and accepted cost spelled out well. **F4 — Medium. The two tenets that collide here are unordered.** `docs/high-level-design.md:20-22` lists three tenets, and two of them pull against each other on this exact choice: "a submitted job is a promise" versus "The mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small." Adding durable queue persistence is the obvious cost the second tenet resists. With three tenets that can conflict and no stated precedence, the reader does the tie-breaking themselves — the thing tenets exist to prevent. Under *tenets capture forward-looking preference*, the fix is either to order them (say which wins when promise and small-hardware meet) or to name the specific exception.
