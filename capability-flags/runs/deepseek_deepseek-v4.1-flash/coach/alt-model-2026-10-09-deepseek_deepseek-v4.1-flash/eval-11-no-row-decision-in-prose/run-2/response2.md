**F1 — High. The in-memory queue silently breaks the promise tenet under an operating condition the docs themselves describe.** `job-queue-design.md:14` says the queue is held "entirely in process memory" and that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them," while the same doc (`:10`) notes the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." The HLD's own tenet (`high-level-design.md:20`) is "A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," and the goal (`:26`) is "Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." Under the described condition — an overnight OS update or power blip — accepted work vanishes and the member must be present to notice and resubmit; the mechanism defeats the goal precisely when the member is absent. Under *tenet cascade* — the design should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean — this is a conflict with no exception recorded. Since the two share no wording, it is exactly the kind of gap that survives a casual reading, so it's worth checking yourself too. The remedies are yours to pick: change the design (persist the queue, or at least the accepted-job list, to the SQLite store the registry already uses), record the exception in the Decisions table naming the tenet traded against and why the modest-hardware constraint wins, or revise the tenet to say best-effort. Note that recording the exception also closes F2 below.

**F2 — High. The durability choice is load-bearing and uncaptured.** `job-queue-design.md:27-33` records three decisions — scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size — all with alternatives and accepted costs, but the in-memory-vs-persistent queue choice, which governs the tenet conflict in F1, has no row. Under *decision capture* — a doc carries current intent, and a choice a cold reader would still question belongs on the record — a reader landing on this design will ask "why would you drop accepted jobs?" and find nothing. The remedy here is the smallest one that settles it: a fuller Decisions row naming the rejected alternative (a durable queue) and the accepted cost (complexity on the mini-PC, or restart-recovery logic). A full decision doc would only be warranted if you want the whole option space — e.g., a write-ahead file vs. SQLite — argued on the record for future readers to re-run.

**F3 — Medium. The behaviors around this are under-specified in EARS.** The design states several behavioral rules the specs don't carry: accept-only-if-the-target-class-has-a-registered-printer (`:20`), content-hash file storage and cheap resubmission (`:14`), the restart-loss behavior itself (`:14`), and the two-priority-class model (`:25`). Under *effective intent-tree alignment* — every LLD behavior should trace to a spec, and every spec to a behavior — these are unnamed intent: nothing to test against, nothing for a future session to grep. Of these, the restart-loss behavior is the one that most needs a spec, because once F1's remedy lands (persist or record) the spec is what fixes the chosen behavior so it can't drift. Consider adding `QUEUE-006` (or a small block) covering whichever restart behavior you choose.

**F4 — Medium. "Members resubmit them" has no mechanism.** Even setting aside F1, `:14` assumes members learn their job was lost, but the design and specs describe no notification, email, or queue-view surface that would tell them. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — a future reader who wasn't in the conversation must be able to act — a cold reader can't tell how resubmission is triggered. If you keep the best-effort design, name the mechanism; if you fix F1, this resolves with it.
