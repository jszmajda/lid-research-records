Two findings sit on the in-memory-queue choice — F1 (the tenet/goal conflict) and F2 (the missing decision capture). Before the detail: the surrounding design is genuinely strong — the HLD's "a submitted job is a promise" tenet and the registry's SQLite precedent are both clear and deliberate, which is exactly why this one choice stands out enough to be worth settling.

**F1 — High. The in-memory queue quietly trades away the project's core promise.** In `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14` the accepted queue is "held entirely in process memory" and jobs "accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them" — while the same file's Context (line 10) says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." Put that beside the HLD: a member uploads in the evening and walks away (`docs/high-level-design.md:20`, "A submitted job is a promise"), a power blip restarts the service overnight, the job is gone, and nobody is present to resubmit it — so the "overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present" goal (HLD:26) fails on the exact scenario the system was built for. No Decisions row names that as an exception. Under *tenets capture forward-looking preference* — tenets and goals are the tie-breaking lean a future session follows when no spec anticipated the choice, so leaf mechanisms shouldn't work against them — this one does, under a condition the LLD itself describes. What makes it a live design concern rather than a harmless tension inside the HLD: the "service stays small" tenet (HLD:22) doesn't force in-memory storage, because `printer-registry-design.md:14` already persists the registry to "a single SQLite file" that "Fits the mini-PC" — durability on this hardware is proven feasible, so the choice reads as technically questionable, not merely a trade-off. Why it matters: a future session loading only the job-queue LLD will implement and defend loss-on-restart as if it were intended, and the promise erodes precisely on the unattended overnight runs that motivated the whole system; the conflict stays invisible until a member arrives to an empty build plate. For the remedy, the choice is yours among three — change the design (persist accepted jobs), record the exception (a Decisions row in `job-queue-design.md` naming the tenet/goal traded against and why), or revise the tenet; I'd hold off picking until you've read F2, because the two converge.

**F2 — High. The durability choice is never captured as a decision.** The same choice lives only in the Architecture prose of `job-queue-design.md:14`; the Decisions & Alternatives table (lines 29–33) has three rows — scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size limit — and each names a rejected alternative and an accepted cost ("Shortest-job-first rejected…", "slugs recycle after 90 days", "keeps the mini-PC disk safe"), but there is no row for holding the queue in memory or dropping jobs on restart, so neither the alternative (persist the queue) nor the cost (members resubmit) is written down. Even "resubmitting the same file is cheap" (line 14) only makes sense in a world where resubmission is expected — context that lived in the conversation that produced the doc and never reached the page. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* — a doc must stand alone for a reader with no memory of the chat that wrote it, and the keep-side places load-bearing choices in the Decisions table with the alternative given up and the cost accepted — a cold reader holding the HLD's promise tenet and this LLD would immediately ask "why are accepted jobs lost?" and be tempted to reverse it, yet can't reconstruct what was traded away. Why it matters: this is exactly the implicit-context leak the cold-read discipline exists to catch; left as prose, every future session re-derives or contradicts the decision, and the team can't tell whether losing jobs is a reviewed trade-off or an accident frozen into the design. The smallest capture that settles it is a fuller Decisions row in `job-queue-design.md` naming the rejected alternative (persist accepted jobs, e.g., to the SQLite store the registry already uses) and the accepted cost (resubmission after a restart); decision docs are rare and reserved for choices that warrant a debate on the record, so a row is enough here.

**What I'd recommend doing.** Both findings sit on one choice, so one small move can close F2 and satisfy the "record the exception" option for F1: a single fuller Decisions row that names the rejected alternative and accepted cost *and* names the tenet/goal traded against with the reason. That's the lowest-risk first step, and the one I'd recommend for the capture gap — it makes the trade-off visible and reviewable before anyone changes behavior. Whether "record the exception" is the right *substantive* answer to the tenet conflict is your call among the three remedies above; if the written-down trade-off doesn't hold up (and the registry's SQLite precedent suggests it may not), the other two are change-the-design or revise-the-tenet.
