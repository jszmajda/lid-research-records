Here's the detail on both findings tied to this issue — they're two sides of the same choice.

**F1 — High. The job queue's in-memory design can quietly break the HLD's own promise to members.** `job-queue-design.md`'s Architecture section states the queue is "held entirely in process memory" and that on restart, "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The same doc's Context names the exact condition that triggers this: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." So a member can upload a job in the evening, the mini-PC restarts overnight for an update, and the job silently vanishes — no notification, no record — right in the unattended window the HLD's Goals section describes as the whole point ("evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present"). This is a **tenet cascade** finding: the HLD's tenet "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away" is a forward-looking lean the design nodes are supposed to honor, and this mechanism works against it under a condition the project's own docs call out as real and routine — not a hypothetical edge case. Left as-is, the cost isn't just this one gap: a tenet that the design silently violates stops functioning as a tie-breaker for future decisions, because nobody can trust it describes what the system actually does. The remedy here is genuinely a three-way choice, and it's yours to make, not mine: **change the design** (e.g., persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs to the on-disk content-hash store that already exists for uploaded files, so a restart can replay them), **record the exception** (add a Decisions & Alternatives row in `job-queue-design.md` naming the tenet traded away and why — e.g., "durability rejected: mini-PC has no reliable persistence layer yet; accepted cost: job loss on restart, mitigated by resubmission"), or **revise the tenet** if "upload and walk away" was never meant to survive a restart. Any of the three is a legitimate LID move; which one fits depends on how often restarts actually happen and how much that failure mode would cost in practice.

**F2 — Medium. That same tradeoff has no row in the Decisions & Alternatives table.** The three existing rows in `job-queue-design.md` (scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size limit) each name a rejected alternative and an accepted cost — the table is otherwise doing its job well. But the in-memory-vs-durability choice, which is at least as consequential as any of those three, isn't captured anywhere; a cold reader hits the "jobs...are gone" line in Architecture with no record of why that was acceptable. This is a **decision-capture** finding (under *docs carry current intent* — docs should read as a fresh author's statement of current intent, not leave load-bearing choices implicit): the missing row means every future session that touches durability has to re-derive or guess at the reasoning instead of reading it. Here's the shortcut: if you pick "record the exception" as the remedy for F1, that single Decisions row closes both findings at once — you don't need to treat them as separate pieces of work. If you instead choose to change the design, F2 mostly dissolves since there's no tradeoff left to document.
