**F1 — High. The job-queue's in-memory queue works against the HLD's own "promise" tenet.** `job-queue-design.md`'s Context section states plainly that "the service runs on the workshop mini-PC, which takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip" — then the Architecture section holds the queue "entirely in process memory," so "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Put those two sentences side by side and the HLD's first tenet — *a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away* — doesn't hold under the exact condition the LLD itself names as routine, not exceptional. This is a **tenet cascade** finding: the question isn't whether the mechanism is buggy, it's whether the design node leans the way the HLD's tenets point, and here it leans away, under a described operating condition, with no record of why that's acceptable. Left as-is, this is the kind of gap that's invisible until it bites — a restart during a plausible overnight window (which is also the project's primary use case: "upload and walk away" happens in the evening, dispatch happens overnight) silently drops a member's job, and nothing in the docs tells a future agent whether that's known-and-accepted or missed. Fixing it now, before code exists, is far cheaper than discovering it after a member loses a print.

**F2 — High. The same tradeoff has no Decisions & Alternatives row.** The other three choices in this LLD — scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size limit — are each captured with the alternative that was rejected and the cost that was accepted (e.g., "UUIDs rejected: unreadable on printer LCDs… accepted cost: slugs recycle after 90 days"). The in-memory-queue choice gets no such row; it's stated once, in Architecture prose, as a fact about the system rather than a decision with a counterfactual. Under *docs carry current intent* — specifically the decision-capture ceiling — a cold reader holding this LLD plus the HLD's tenets would still want to reconstruct what was given up (durability across restarts) and why (presumably: simplicity, no persistence layer needed on modest hardware) — and right now they can't, because it was never written down as a choice.

**Recommendation.** F1 and F2 are the same underlying gap, so one fix closes both. You have three honest options, and I'd leave the pick to you rather than push one:

1. **Change the design** — persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs (even something as simple as a flat file or a table in the registry's existing SQLite store) so a restart doesn't drop them. This actually satisfies the tenet rather than just documenting around it.
2. **Record the exception** — add a Decisions & Alternatives row in `job-queue-design.md` naming the rejected alternative (a durable queue) and the accepted cost (jobs lost on restart; members resubmit), and note explicitly that this trades against the HLD's "promise" tenet. This doesn't change behavior, but it makes the tradeoff visible and deliberate instead of silent — and it closes F2 automatically, since the row *is* the capture.
3. **Revise the tenet** — if, on reflection, "walk away" was never meant to survive a restart, soften the tenet's wording so it doesn't overclaim.

If you go with option 1, know that it may pull against the HLD's third tenet — "the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small" — since durability usually means a bit more moving machinery. That's worth a glance when you pick the mechanism (even a lightweight file-per-job spool on disk, next to the already-content-hashed G-code files, would likely satisfy both tenets without much weight). Once you've decided, this is a docs edit you can make directly — no `/update-lid` needed, since it's a content change to an existing LLD, not a configuration one.
