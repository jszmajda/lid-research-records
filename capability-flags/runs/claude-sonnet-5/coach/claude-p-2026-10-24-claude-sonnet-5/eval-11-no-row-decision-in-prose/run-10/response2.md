Here's the detailed walkthrough of that thread — it's really three findings that share one root cause, so I'll take them together.

**F1 — High. The queue's durability model runs against its own "promise" tenet.** `job-queue-design.md`'s Architecture section holds the queue "entirely in process memory" and is explicit about the consequence: "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." That's not a hypothetical edge case — the same doc's Context section names the exact trigger: the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." So the condition under which jobs vanish is a condition the design itself says happens routinely. Set that against the HLD's first tenet: "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away." That's the scenario the whole product exists for — "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup, with no member present" is literally Goal #1. An unattended OS update at 2am that silently drops an accepted job is precisely the failure the tenet was written to rule out, and the member has no way to know it happened until they show up the next morning to nothing. Under *tenet cascade* — do the design nodes lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals say to lean? — this is a real conflict, not just a cost against a goal: it's a described condition where the mechanism actively defeats the tenet.

**F2 — High. That tradeoff was never written down.** The Decisions & Alternatives table in `job-queue-design.md` has three rows — scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size limit — and none of them is "should the queue survive a restart." That's the kind of decision a cold reader would stop on: given tenet #1, why is the queue in-memory at all? Under *docs carry current intent* (its keep-side: a decision that stays live once landed needs the row that lets a future reader reconstruct what was given up and why), this is under-captured. Right now the only way to learn this tradeoff exists is to read the Architecture prose carefully and notice the tension yourself — the next session (or the next contributor) won't necessarily do that before building on top of the current design.

**F4 — Medium. The tenets that are pulling against each other aren't ordered.** The HLD has three tenets and no stated precedence. Tenet #1 ("a submitted job is a promise") leans toward durability. Tenet #3 ("the mini-PC is modest hardware; the service stays small") leans toward staying lightweight — and a durable queue (disk-backed journal, SQLite-backed job table, etc.) is more machinery than an in-memory list. These two are exactly what collided in F1. Under *tenets capture forward-looking preference*, the whole point of a tenet is to pre-narrow a lean so a future session doesn't have to guess — but with two tenets in tension and no order, whoever implemented the queue had to pick one implicitly, and picked "stay small" without that being a visible, endorsed choice.

**Recommendation.** Per the coach's remedies for a tenet conflict, this is the team's call among three options, not something I'd pick for you:

1. **Change the design** — give the queue durability across restart (e.g., persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs to disk, replay on startup), which would satisfy tenet #1 without necessarily contradicting tenet #3 if the persistence stays lightweight (a flat file or the registry's existing SQLite file, not a new service).
2. **Record the exception** — if losing queued jobs on restart is actually acceptable (restarts are rare enough, or members already expect to double-check), add a Decisions & Alternatives row in `job-queue-design.md` naming tenet #1 as the thing traded away and why the cost is accepted. Doing this would close F2 at the same time — a recorded exception *is* the capture.
3. **Revise the tenet** — if "upload and walk away" was aspirational rather than a hard guarantee, soften it so it doesn't overclaim.

Whichever you pick, I'd also suggest ordering the three tenets (or at least noting which wins when they conflict) so the next ambiguous case doesn't repeat this — that's the F4 fix and it's cheap regardless of which of the three options above you take.
