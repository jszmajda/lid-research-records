**F1 — High. The in-memory queue drops accepted jobs on restart, which works against the "job is a promise" tenet.** `job-queue-design.md`'s Architecture section says: "On startup the service rebuilds its picture of the farm by polling each registered printer for the job it is currently running; jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." The HLD's tenet states "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," and its goal is "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." Both name the exact condition that breaks here: the HLD itself says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip," and under that condition, a job sitting accepted-but-undispatched silently disappears — the member has to notice and resubmit, which is precisely the chat-and-hover workflow the HLD opens by saying the service exists to replace. Under *tenet cascade* — design nodes should lean the way the HLD's tenets and goals point, since nothing else will catch a clash a future session might miss — this is the kind of gap that's invisible until a printer farm restarts overnight with the queue full, and by then it's a support complaint, not a design review comment. Catching it now, before dispatch code exists, is far cheaper than after.

**F2 — Medium. That same restart behavior isn't recorded as a deliberate trade-off anywhere.** The Decisions & Alternatives table in `job-queue-design.md` has three rows (scheduling order, job identifiers, upload size limit) but none for "why do accepted-but-undispatched jobs not survive a restart." Under *docs carry current intent* — the keep-side that protects rationale, not just residue — a choice a cold reader would still question needs to be reconstructable from the record: what was rejected, and what cost was accepted. Right now a future session reading this LLD has no way to tell whether dropping those jobs was a deliberate, considered trade-off or an oversight.

**What I'd recommend:** this is one underlying issue showing up as two findings, and fixing F1 well closes F2 along with it. Three paths, your call:

1. **Change the design** — persist accepted-but-undispatched jobs (e.g., write them to the same content-addressed disk store already used for G-code, replay on startup alongside the printer poll) so the tenet actually holds. This is the most direct fix if "upload and walk away" is meant literally.
2. **Keep the current behavior, but record it as a deliberate exception** — add a Decisions & Alternatives row naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g., "durability across restarts rejected: adds a persistence layer the mini-PC doesn't otherwise need; accepted cost: a job accepted-but-not-dispatched before a restart is lost and must be resubmitted"). This alone resolves F2, and is a legitimate answer to F1 if the team decides the simplicity is worth the occasional lost job.
3. **Revise the tenet** — if "upload and walk away" was never meant to survive a crash, narrow its wording so it stops promising something the design doesn't deliver.

I'd lean toward option 1 or 2 rather than 3 — the tenet reads like it's doing real work elsewhere in the design (it's part of why the service exists at all), so narrowing it should be a last resort.
