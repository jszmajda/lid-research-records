Happy to dig in — this one is worth more than my report gave it credit for.

**The observation.** The job-queue LLD's Architecture section (docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md:14) says the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and that after a restart, "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched… are gone, and members resubmit them." What makes this notable is that it appears nowhere in the Decisions & Alternatives table — that table covers scheduling order, job IDs, and upload limits, but the single most surprising choice in the leaf (accepted work evaporates on reboot) survives only as prose. Under *decision capture* — a choice a cold reader would question or try to reverse should have its reasoning on the record — this reads like an under-captured decision: a reader holding the docs above it would absolutely ask "why would we lose members' submitted jobs?"

**The tenet tension.** This is also where the design leans against your own first tenet: "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away." The mechanism only keeps that promise while the mini-PC stays up — and your own Context section says the machine "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." The docs never share wording on this, which is exactly the shape of a tenet conflict (*tenets capture forward-looking preference*, dimension 19): under the operating condition these very docs describe, the design works against the tenet. The partial mitigation is real — G-code is on disk under a content hash, so resubmission is cheap — but "cheap for the member who happens to notice" isn't "walk away"; nothing in the design tells a member their job died overnight.

**Three remedies, your choice.** You could **change the design** — persisting the queue as a small SQLite table next to the registry store would close the gap and costs little on this hardware; that's the strongest fix for the tenet. You could **record the exception** — a Decisions row (or fuller) naming the tenet traded against, why in-memory was acceptable (e.g., queue states are derived from printers anyway, and disk-persistence would complicate the rebuild logic) — that's legitimate if the overnight-loss window is genuinely rare and small. Or you could **revise the tenet** if "walk away" was overpromised and you'd rather commit to "the queue explains itself, including when it loses a job" — which would also suggest a new spec (something like: when the service restarts with undelivered accepted jobs, the system SHALL report them as expired in the queue view).

My recommendation: persist the queue. It's the option where the fix costs less than the explanation of not fixing it, and it also closes the decision-capture gap — the Decisions row then records *why* SQLite-backed (the alternative being in-memory, with the resubmission cost you're currently accepting by default). Worth noting the design concern is its own finding apart from the capture gap: even a perfect row can't make a lost job feel like a promise kept.
