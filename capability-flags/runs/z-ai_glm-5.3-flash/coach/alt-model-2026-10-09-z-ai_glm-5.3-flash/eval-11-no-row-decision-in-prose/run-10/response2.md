Here's the walkthrough of both findings — they're really one choice seen from two angles.

**F2 (high) — the in-memory queue trades away your first tenet under a condition your own docs say to expect.** `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md` §Architecture says the queue is "held entirely in process memory," and the very next sentence accepts that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Meanwhile the HLD's first tenet is "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away," the headline goal is "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present," and the LLD's own Context section says the mini-PC "takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." So the mechanism defeats the tenet precisely under a condition the docs describe as normal, and nothing on the page acknowledges the trade — under *tenets capture forward-looking preference* (a tenet pre-narrows the decisions no spec anticipated), the conflict should either not exist or be on the record. Why it matters: the failure mode is silent and lands on your product promise — a member uploads at 8pm, the mini-PC patches at 3am, the job evaporates, and nobody knows until the morning pickup fails. Tenet conflicts that share no wording with the mechanism are exactly the ones future sessions walk past, because each doc reads fine in isolation. There are three remedies, and the choice is yours: **(1) change the design** — persist accepted jobs, most naturally next to the content-hashed G-code you already keep on disk, which is a small change at this scale; **(2) record the exception** — a Decisions & Alternatives row naming the tenet traded against and why (e.g., restarts are rare, members are reachable to resubmit, persistence complexity avoided, accepted cost: undispatched jobs vanish on restart); or **(3) revise the tenet** — if losing undispatched jobs is genuinely fine, say what the promise actually covers ("an accepted job is dispatched or visibly failed"). If you take option 2, it also closes F3 in the same stroke.

**F3 (medium) — that same choice is the one big call with no Decisions row.** The `job-queue` Decisions & Alternatives table records three choices in the house style — chosen, rejected alternative, accepted cost (FIFO over shortest-job-first, slugs over UUIDs, 64 MB limit) — but the persistence choice survives only as prose in §Architecture. Under *docs carry current intent* — rationale and considered alternatives are present intent, and the ceiling on recording them is whether a cold reader would still question the choice — this one fails the test precisely because the docs above it argue the other way: a fresh reader holding the tenet and the Context section would want to reverse the in-memory design, and the table gives them nothing telling them it was weighed. That's the decision-capture gap: the most consequential choice in the node is the only one whose reasoning isn't on the record, so a future session can't tell whether it was deliberate or never questioned — and might "fix" it, silently reversing intent. Try the smallest capture that settles it: one row, e.g. *Queue persistence | In-process memory, accepted jobs lost on restart | Durable queue (SQLite or disk journal) rejected: [the actual reason]; accepted cost: members resubmit undispatched jobs after a restart*. A decision doc is not warranted here — nothing else in the docs sizes itself around this choice — the row is the right weight. And note the sequencing: if you choose remedy 1 for F2 and persist the queue instead, this row stops being needed (the decision mutates into the new design); if you keep in-memory, the row is the fix for both findings at once.
