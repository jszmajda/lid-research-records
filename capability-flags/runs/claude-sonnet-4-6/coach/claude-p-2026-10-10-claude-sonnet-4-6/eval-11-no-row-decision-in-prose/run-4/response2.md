**F1 — High. The job-queue's in-memory design silently contradicts the project's first tenet, and there's no record of that tradeoff.**

The job-queue-design.md describes the queue as "held entirely in process memory" and states plainly that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." That's a deliberate and reasonable choice for a mini-PC service — but the HLD's first tenet is "a submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away." A member who uploads a job in the evening and comes in the morning to collect their part has no way to know the mini-PC took an OS update at 2am and dropped their job. The promise is broken, and the doc says so implicitly, but nothing in the design layer acknowledges it.

The two principles at play here are *tenet cascade* — design nodes should lean the way the HLD's tenets say to lean, and exceptions should be explicitly on the record — and *decision capture* — a cold reader holding the job-queue LLD alongside the HLD would immediately be puzzled by this contradiction, which means the reasoning that settled it exists only in someone's head (or a past chat session). That reasoning is exactly the kind of thing LID is designed to preserve. If it's not written down, the next session that touches the queue will either re-open the debate or — worse — quietly change the design thinking it's fixing a bug.

The good news is this doesn't require changing the design. The Decisions & Alternatives table in `job-queue-design.md` is the right place to record it: a row capturing the choice (in-memory vs. durable queue), what was rejected, and the cost accepted. Something like: "Durable job store rejected — adds write-ahead complexity the mini-PC hardware budget doesn't justify; accepted cost: jobs queued but not dispatched before a restart are lost and must be resubmitted." That one row closes both findings — it captures the decision *and* records the exception against the tenet.

That said, the three remedies here are yours to choose between: **change the design** (add persistence and honor the promise fully), **record the exception** (a Decisions row naming the tenet traded and why, which closes both F1 and makes F4 about tenet ordering less urgent), or **revise the tenet** (if the promise was always meant as best-effort, the tenet is overstating the intent). Recording the exception is the lightest-weight path and the most likely to reflect what was actually decided.
