These two findings are closely related — the same design choice generates both — so it helps to read them together.

**F1 — High. The in-memory queue works against the "submitted job is a promise" tenet under the operating conditions the doc itself describes.**

The job-queue LLD's Architecture section states plainly: "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." That's the mechanism. The Context section, one paragraph above it, describes the operating environment: "the service runs on the workshop mini-PC, which takes unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip." And the HLD's primary goal is "evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present." Put these together and the conflict is clear: a member uploads in the evening, the mini-PC takes an OS update at 2am, the queue is empty at 3am when the printers go idle, and the member arrives in the morning to find nothing was printed — precisely the failure the tenet exists to prevent. Under *tenet cascade* — each design node should lean the way the HLD's tenets say to lean — a mechanism that defeats a tenet under described conditions should either be changed, or have an explicit exception recorded explaining what was traded and why. Right now there's no exception on record, so a future agent reading these docs has no way to know this was a deliberate trade rather than something that slipped through.

For remedy, you have three choices, and it's genuinely your call: change the design (persist the queue so jobs survive restarts), record the exception (a Decisions row in job-queue-design.md naming the "job is a promise" tenet, what cost made you accept it — perhaps "persistence complexity doesn't fit the modest-hardware tenet"), or revise the tenet (narrow it: "a submitted job is best-effort on a single-node service; members should check status before walking away"). All three are defensible. The coach can't pick for you because the right answer depends on how seriously you mean the evening-upload goal. If overnight reliability is load-bearing, the design needs to change. If the mini-PC's simplicity matters more, the tenet needs to reflect that honestly.

**F2 — High. The choice to hold the queue in process memory is absent from the Decisions table.**

This is a separate finding from the tenet conflict, though the same design choice feeds both. The job-queue LLD makes a significant architectural decision — no persistence, queue rebuilt from scratch on restart — and surfaces it only in the Architecture prose. It appears as a statement of fact ("held entirely in process memory") rather than a recorded decision, so the *why* is nowhere. Under *decision capture* — choices that a cold reader would question or try to reverse should have their reasoning on the page — this one doesn't pass: any future session reading the Architecture section will encounter a notable constraint without the reasoning that produced it. They'll either re-derive a justification (probably "mini-PC is modest hardware"), accept the constraint without question, or work around it, and in none of those cases does the actual reasoning shape what they do.

The fix here is a Decisions row in `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md` — something like: rejected alternative (persist the queue to SQLite or disk), chosen approach (in-process memory), rationale (whatever actually drove it — simplicity, the modest-hardware tenet, or a considered view that lost jobs are acceptable). You don't need a full decision doc for this; a well-populated row is the right size. If you decide to also record the tenet exception (from F1), that row can do double duty — naming the tenet traded against closes both findings at once.
