**F2 — High. The in-memory job queue mechanism directly contradicts the upload-and-walk-away tenet and overnight pickup goal under documented workshop conditions.** In `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md` (§ Context, § Architecture), the mini-PC is explicitly expected to take "unattended OS updates and the occasional power blip," yet the queue is held "entirely in process memory" such that "jobs that were accepted but not yet dispatched before a restart are gone, and members resubmit them." Under *tenet cascade* — leaf design mechanisms must lean the way upstream HLD tenets and goals lean under the operating conditions described — this mechanism actively defeats the HLD's core tenet ("A submitted job is a promise: members should be able to upload and walk away") and primary goal ("Evening upload, overnight print, morning pickup — with no member present"). If a member uploads at 21:00 and walks away expecting morning pickup, an unattended 03:00 OS update drops their job silently without printing, breaking the core covenant of the system. Leaving this unresolved means downstream code and tests will bake in volatile queue handling that betrays member expectations and forces future sessions to untangle conflicting requirements across the arrow.

**F3 — High. Storing the accepted job queue entirely in volatile memory is an uncaptured architectural decision.** In `docs/intent/job-queue/job-queue-design.md`, the Decisions & Alternatives table records choices for scheduling order, job identifiers, and upload limits, but omits the choice to keep the queue in memory and lose accepted jobs across restarts. Under *docs carry current intent, written to be read cold* (specifically *decision capture* — choices that stay live once landed must record what was chosen, what was rejected, and the accepted cost), an architectural choice that leaves members vulnerable to data loss is one a cold reader will immediately question or attempt to reverse, especially when `printer-registry` already uses SQLite for persistent storage on the same mini-PC. Leaving the reasoning off the page leaves future agents guessing whether in-memory storage was a conscious trade-off for mini-PC simplicity or an accidental omission, compounding drift when persistence is inevitably revisited.

### What I recommend doing

When an LLD mechanism conflicts with an HLD tenet or goal, LID offers three remedies. You can choose which path best reflects the team's true intent:

1. **Change the design (align mechanism to tenet):** Persist accepted jobs across restarts so the promise holds. Because `printer-registry` already maintains a single SQLite file on the mini-PC, `job-queue` could persist queue state to SQLite or an append-only log on disk beside the hashed G-code files, recovering queued jobs on startup instead of dropping them. (This also clarifies how the 90-day slug lifetime noted in F8 survives restarts.)
2. **Record the exception (keep the design, document the trade-off):** If keeping the service small and stateless in memory is paramount on this hardware, keep the in-memory queue but add a row to `job-queue-design.md`'s Decisions & Alternatives table. The row should explicitly name the tenet traded against ("A submitted job is a promise"), the rejected alternative (SQLite / disk persistence), the rationale (minimizing state and disk writes on modest hardware), and the accepted cost (members must resubmit jobs if the mini-PC reboots before dispatch). Recording this exception also resolves finding F3.
3. **Revise the tenet or goal (align upstream intent to reality):** If unattended overnight reliability was never meant to survive host restarts, soften the tenet in `docs/high-level-design.md` (for instance, clarifying that the promise applies once a print has commenced on a printer, but queue presence is best-effort across host reboots) and adjust the morning pickup goal accordingly.
